Extracting Shocks for the New Keynesian Model

The canonical New Keynesian model is driven by four shocks: to technology, to the weight households put on the disutility of work, to their discount factor (demand) and to monetary policy. Each needs a persistence and a size, and the model says nothing about either. This page backs the four shocks out of US data, step by step, measures their persistence and size, and asks whether they behave as the model assumes: persistent but independent of one another, and, for monetary policy, in line with the episodes that the Federal Reserve’s own records identify as policy shocks (Romer and Romer, 2023).

Step 1 Output, inflation and the interest rate are not enough

The natural way to back out the shocks is to use the variables the model is about: output, inflation and the interest rate. With three shocks, as in the notes, three series would pin them down exactly each quarter. But technology and the weight on the disutility of labour enter the model in only one place, real marginal cost, \(\hat{\mu}_t = \hat{\theta}_t + (\eta+\sigma)\hat{y}_t - (1+\eta)\hat{a}_t\), and through it flexible-price output. A 1 percent rise in technology therefore has exactly the same effect on output, inflation and the interest rate as a fall of \(1+\eta = 6\) percent in the weight on the disutility of labour, whenever the two have the same persistence, as in the notes:

The two columns are proportional, so the matrix linking the three series to the three shocks has a zero determinant and cannot be inverted. With different persistences, the split would rest on that difference alone, and would be very imprecise. What separates the two shocks is their effect on hours worked: more technology raises output per hour, a lower willingness to work does not. So the model needs one more series, labour productivity, and one more shock to keep the system exactly identified. The shock added is a shock to the discount factor, the standard demand shock of the New Keynesian model (Galí, 2015, Chapter 3).

Step 2 Measure technology with labour productivity

In the model, firms produce with labour only, \(Y_t = A_t h_t\), so technology is output per hour: \(\hat{a}_t = \hat{y}_t - \hat{h}_t\). Its data counterpart is the HP cyclical component of output per hour in the nonfarm business sector, which gives the technology state directly, without using the rest of the model. The chart compares it with John Fernald’s utilization-adjusted total factor productivity, which corrects measured productivity for capital and for the intensity with which labour and capital are used (Basu, Fernald and Kimball, 2006).

The two measures move together but are far from identical. Labour productivity is procyclical, while utilization-adjusted TFP is roughly acyclical: part of what the model calls technology is the more intensive use of labour and capital in booms, which a model without capital or variable effort cannot separate from technology.

Step 3 Back out the other three shocks

Given the solution of the model, the four observables are linear in the four states, \(x_t = M z_t\), with \(x_t = (\hat{y}_t,\ \hat{y}_t - \hat{h}_t,\ \pi_t,\ \hat{R}_t)'\) and \(z_t = (\hat{a}_t,\ \hat{\theta}_t,\ \hat{\zeta}_t,\ \nu_t)'\). Each column of \(M\) gives the effect of one state; the row for labour productivity is \((1, 0, 0, 0)\), since it is technology. The matrix depends on the persistences of the states, here those of Step 4:

Inverting it, \(z_t = M^{-1} x_t\) quarter by quarter: given technology, output, inflation and the interest rate determine the weight on the disutility of labour, the discount factor and the monetary policy shock. The states reproduce the four series exactly.

The monetary policy shock is the gap between the federal funds rate and the rate prescribed by the rule given the backed-out economy: positive values are a tighter policy than the rule. Its largest negative values, in 2021–22, are the quarters when the Federal Reserve kept its rate near zero while inflation rose. Hollow circles mark the narrative dates of Romer and Romer (2023), discussed in Step 6.

Step 4 Persistences consistent with the shocks

The matrix \(M\) depends on the persistences, which the states backed out with it should reproduce. Starting from the persistences of the notes, the states are backed out, an autoregression of order one is estimated on each by ordinary least squares, \(z^k_t = c_k + \rho_k z^k_{t-1} + \varepsilon^k_t\), and the model is solved again with the estimates, until the persistences no longer change:

The persistence of technology does not change, because labour productivity measures technology whatever the other parameters; the other two converge in a few iterations. The monetary shock stays i.i.d., as in the notes (Step 6 shows what happens if it is not).

Step 5 The shocks and their properties

The shocks are the innovations of the four processes, the part of each state that its own past does not predict, \(\varepsilon^k_t = z^k_t - \rho_k z^k_{t-1}\); the monetary shock, i.i.d., is its own innovation. Their standard deviations are the sizes of the shocks in the model.

Properties of the states and the shocks

Technology, the labour-disutility weight and the discount factor in percent; the monetary policy shock in percentage points at an annual rate. * Galí (2015, Chapter 3); the notes have no demand shock.

None of the shocks is normally distributed. The labour-preference shock is dominated by a few very large values, above all in 2020, when output collapsed while output per hour rose: the model can only read the fall in hours as households suddenly unwilling to work. Recessions come with negative technology and demand shocks and positive labour-preference shocks.

Step 6 Do the shocks behave as the model assumes?

Are the shocks independent?

The model treats the four shocks as independent sources of fluctuations, which is what makes it possible to ask what each of them did. Nothing in the inversion imposes it, so their correlations are a test of the model:

The monetary policy shock is essentially uncorrelated with the others, as the model assumes. The three non-monetary shocks are not: the labour-preference and demand shocks move strongly in opposite directions, and both are correlated with technology. They are partly picking up the same disturbances, which suggests that the model lacks a source of fluctuations, such as a cost-push shock, wage rigidity or investment, that the three are sharing between them (Ireland, 2004; Smets and Wouters, 2007).

Is the monetary shock i.i.d.?

The notes assume that monetary policy shocks last one quarter. The backed-out shock is instead very persistent (see “AR(1) of \(\nu_t\)” in Step 7): the Federal Reserve moves its rate gradually, while the rule in the model responds at once to inflation and the output gap, so the gap between the two persists. This is the interest-rate smoothing that estimated rules capture with a lagged interest rate (Clarida, Galí and Gertler, 2000). Making the shock persistent instead (the last row of Step 7) is not a remedy: it makes the monetary and demand shocks strongly correlated, because both then describe slow movements in the interest rate.

The Romer and Romer narrative dates

Romer and Romer (2023) read the records of the Federal Open Market Committee and identify ten episodes in which the Federal Reserve deliberately changed policy to move the economy, rather than in response to it: nine contractions and one expansion. They are an external check that uses no model. If the backed-out shock captures monetary policy, it should rise around a contraction and fall around the expansion. Since the dates are months and the data quarterly averages, a shock late in a quarter shows partly in the next one, so the table reports the change over two quarters as well.

The agreement is good from 1978, for the Volcker disinflation and the tightening of 1988, but weak before: until the 1970s the Federal Reserve steered money-market conditions more broadly rather than the federal funds rate, so the rate, and the shock backed out from it, reflect its decisions less closely. The continuous Romer and Romer (2004) series, updated by Wieland and Yang (2020), would allow a quarter-by-quarter comparison; see Variants and background.

Step 7 Robustness

The whole procedure, Steps 3 to 5, repeated with other choices: HP-filtered rather than demeaned inflation and interest rate, Rotemberg rather than Calvo pricing (with the notes’ \(\omega = 75\), which gives almost the same Phillips curve), two subsamples before and during the Great Moderation, without the quarters when the federal funds rate was at its lower bound (an average below 0.25 percent: 2009–15 and 2020–21), which the linear rule ignores, and with a persistent monetary shock.

The persistences are broadly stable across samples and methods; the sizes of the shocks are not. All are much smaller during the Great Moderation, and the labour-preference shock shrinks by more than a third without the lower-bound quarters, which include 2020. The correlation between the labour-preference and demand shocks changes sign across subsamples, another sign that the two are not well separated.

Calibration for the New Keynesian Model

The persistences of Step 4 and the standard deviations of Step 5 are the default calibration of the shocks on The Canonical New Keynesian Model. They are consistent with the structural parameters used here; changing those parameters on the model page changes the backed-out shocks, and these values would then no longer be exactly consistent.

Variants and background

The continuous Romer and Romer series

Romer and Romer (2004) built a continuous series of monetary policy shocks: the change in the intended federal funds rate at each meeting that is not explained by the Federal Reserve’s own Greenbook forecasts. Wieland and Yang (2020) extended it to 2007. It would allow a quarter-by-quarter correlation with the shock backed out here, but it is distributed with a program that has to be run to produce the series, and it ends in 2007; it may be added later. The 2023 dates used above are published in the paper and need no processing.

Method

The model is solved by undetermined coefficients, as on the model page, at the structural parameters of the notes. The observables are in model units: output and labour productivity in percent of trend divided by 100, inflation and the federal funds rate in percent at an annual rate, demeaned (or HP filtered), divided by 400. The autoregressions include a constant and are estimated by ordinary least squares; the standard errors are the usual ones, which ignore that the states are themselves estimated. The fixed point is reached when no persistence changes by more than \(10^{-8}\) between two iterations. Skewness and kurtosis are those of the sample; the Jarque and Bera (1980) statistic tests whether they are those of a normal distribution. In the subsamples, the states are backed out and the persistences estimated on the subsample only.

Data
  • Output: real GDP (GDPC1) per person (B230RC0Q173SBEA), U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, HP cyclical component (λ = 1600) of \(100 \times \ln\).
  • Labour productivity: output per hour in the nonfarm business sector (OPHNFB), U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, HP cyclical component.
  • Inflation: annualised quarterly change in the GDP price index (GDPDEF), \(400 \times \Delta \ln P_t\).
  • Interest rate: effective federal funds rate (FEDFUNDS), Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, quarterly average.
  • Utilization-adjusted TFP: John Fernald, Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco (Fernald, 2014), HP cyclical component of the log level; used only for comparison.

The sample starts in 1954 Q3, the first full quarter of the federal funds rate, and the HP filter is applied to each series on its full history, as on the Business Cycles page.
