Prices, dividends and earnings

Quarterly averages of the monthly S&P Composite index (the S&P 500 from 1957), itself the average of daily closing prices, and of dividends and earnings over the past twelve months per share of the index, with the dividends paid by all US corporations. Real values are deflated by the GDP price index (2017 = 100); the switches at the top of the page choose real or nominal values and add trends.

Valuation ratios

Valuation ratios compare the price of the index with what it pays or earns. High prices relative to dividends or earnings have historically been followed by low returns over the following decade rather than by faster dividend growth (Campbell and Shiller, 1988; Cochrane, 2008, 2011).

Price–dividend ratio (P/D)
The price of the index over its dividends of the past twelve months: how many years of current dividends a share costs.
Dividend–price ratio (D/P)
The inverse, in percent: the dividend yield, the cash return of holding the index at current prices.
Price–earnings ratio (P/E)
The price over earnings of the past twelve months: how many dollars investors pay for one dollar of current annual earnings.
CAPE
The cyclically adjusted price–earnings ratio (Shiller P/E): the real price over the average of real earnings in the previous ten years.

Returns

The return of a quarter is the change in the quarterly average price plus a quarter of the twelve-month dividend, relative to the previous quarter’s price; the capital gain is the change in the price alone.

Growth rates

Average growth rates of prices, dividends and earnings, and average returns, over the latest quarter, the last ten years and the whole sample since 1947, in nominal and real terms.

Definitions, sources and methods

Data

All stock-market series come from Robert J. Shiller’s monthly data (shillerdata.com), the data behind Irrational Exuberance (Shiller, 2015). The price is the monthly average of daily closes of the S&P Composite index (the S&P 500 from March 1957). Dividends and earnings are twelve-month totals per share of the index, interpolated from quarterly figures; they are published a few months after prices, so those series end earlier. The 3-month Treasury bill rate (TB3MS) is from the Federal Reserve via FRED, and real GDP (GDPC1) from the Bureau of Economic Analysis via FRED.

Every series is the average of its monthly values over each complete quarter. Real series divide the quarterly average by the GDP price index (GDPDEF, 2017 = 100, from the Bureau of Economic Analysis via FRED), the deflator of real GDP, so that real stock prices and dividends are measured like the output of the RBC model they are compared with. The CAPE alone is built with the consumer price index in Shiller’s file, to reproduce his published series.

Corporate dividends are the net dividends of US corporations in the national income and product accounts (DIVIDEND, Bureau of Economic Analysis, quarterly, billions of dollars at a seasonally adjusted annual rate). They cover all corporations, listed or not, and are the aggregate counterpart of the dividends in a model economy; per capita, they are divided by the population (B230RC0Q173SBEA). The S&P 500 dividends are per share of the index, so they have no total or per-capita version.

The calculations (trends, growth rates and their averages) use the quarterly data from 1947 Q1, as on Main Economic Indicators; the charts start in 1950 Q1.

Ratios, growth rates and returns
  • Price–dividend ratio: \(P_t / D_t\), the quarterly average price over the quarterly average of twelve-month dividends. The dividend–price ratio is \(100\,D_t / P_t\), in percent. Dividends are measured over twelve months, as in Shiller’s data and Campbell and Shiller (1988); a ratio built with the quarter’s dividend alone, \(D_t/4\), is four times larger and has the same growth rate.
  • Price–earnings ratio: \(P_t / E_t\), the price over twelve-month earnings.
  • CAPE: computed monthly as the real price over the average of real earnings in the ten years ending the previous month, \(p_m / \big(\tfrac{1}{120}\sum_{j=1}^{120} e_{m-j}\big)\) (Campbell and Shiller, 1988, 1998), then averaged over the quarter. The monthly values reproduce Shiller’s published CAPE exactly and are checked against it at every update.
  • Growth rates: \(g_t = 100\,(\ln x_t - \ln x_{t-1})\), percent per quarter; annualised, \(4 g_t\). The growth rate of the dividend–price ratio is \(-g_t\) of the price–dividend ratio, and that of the price–dividend ratio is the growth of prices minus the growth of dividends.
  • Returns: the real total return is \(100\,\big[(p_t + d_t/4)/p_{t-1} - 1\big]\), with \(p\) and \(d\) the real quarterly price and twelve-month dividend; the capital gain uses the price alone. The excess return is the nominal return, computed in the same way, minus the quarter’s average 3-month Treasury bill rate divided by four.

The growth-rate table shows, for each series, the rate of the latest quarter, the average over the last 40 quarters and the average over the whole sample since 1947, in nominal and real terms, per quarter or annualised (four times the quarterly rate; returns are not compounded). Dividends and earnings are published a few months after prices, so their latest quarter can be earlier.

The statistics of growth rates and returns are the standard deviation, the standard deviation relative to that of real GDP growth, the first-order autocorrelation and the correlation with real GDP growth, on the quarters each series shares with GDP, from 1950 Q1; the mean is added for these series.

Trends

The trend switch adds a trend to the charts of prices, dividends and earnings, as on Main Economic Indicators. Each series is filtered as \(100 \times \ln x_t\) from 1947 Q1 and the trend is mapped back to levels: the Hodrick–Prescott trend (λ = 1600; Hodrick and Prescott, 1997), and the Christiano–Fitzgerald trend, the series minus its fluctuations of 6 to 32 quarters (Christiano and Fitzgerald, 2003). The deviations from these trends are the cycles shown on Stock Market Cycles.

Missing data and estimates

Where a source did not publish a value for a period, the value is estimated and flagged rather than left blank, because gaps break time-series and econometric analysis. An isolated gap of up to three consecutive periods is filled by linear interpolation between the published values on either side, in logs for series measured in levels (constant growth across the gap). Estimates in an input also flag the measures built from it. The downloads mark them in an _estimated column (1 = estimated).
