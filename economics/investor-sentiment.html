Investor Sentiment and Equity Issuance

When investors are optimistic, young and hard-to-value firms are priced high, more firms go public and firms raise more equity. This page shows the investor sentiment index of Baker and Wurgler (2006, 2007) and its components, US equity issuance relative to debt and to the market value of corporate equities, and the statistics they give for calibrating models in which new firms and new shares enter the stock market: the persistence of sentiment and the value of new issues relative to the market.

Investor sentiment

Baker and Wurgler (2006, 2007) measure investor sentiment, the propensity to speculate, from market prices and volumes that move with the demand for young, small, unprofitable and hard-to-value stocks. Their index is the first principal component of five such measures, standardised to mean zero and unit variance. Its orthogonalised version removes the part explained by macroeconomic conditions.

Sentiment
optimism or pessimism about stocks not justified by fundamentals; it matters most for firms whose value is hardest to judge.
Index
a low-frequency level indicator: its components are smoothed or lagged twelve months, so its quarter-to-quarter changes should not be read as changes in sentiment.

More on the index and its components

Components of the index

Each component rises with sentiment: more IPOs and higher first-day returns, a larger share of equity in new issues, a smaller discount on closed-end funds, and a lower premium for dividend payers.

IPO
initial public offering: a firm sells shares to the public for the first time; its first-day return is the rise of the price on the first day of trading.
Closed-end fund discount
how far the shares of closed-end funds trade below the value of the stocks they hold.
Dividend premium
the log difference between the average market-to-book ratios of dividend payers and non-payers.

Equity issuance

How much equity firms raise, relative to debt and to the value of existing shares. Gross issues count the new shares sold; net issuance subtracts the shares bought back and those retired in mergers and acquisitions, which since the 1980s often exceed new issues.

Equity share of new issues
equity issues over equity plus debt issues in the past twelve months (Baker and Wurgler, 2000).
Market value
the value at market prices of the shares of US nonfinancial corporations (Federal Reserve, Financial Accounts).

IPOs and the value of entrants

Firms that go public bring their whole equity to the stock market, not only the cash they raise. The market value of each year’s IPOs at their first closing price, relative to the value of the market at the end of the previous year, measures how much of the market is new each year: about 1.4 percent a year since 1980, with peaks in the IPO waves of the early 1980s, the 1990s and 2020–2021.

Market value at the first close
all the firm’s shares after the offering, of all classes, valued at the price at the end of the first day of trading (Ritter).
Proceeds
the cash raised by the shares sold in the offering, a fraction of the firm’s value.

Definitions and methods

The Baker–Wurgler index and its components

Baker and Wurgler (2006) build the index as the first principal component of six proxies for sentiment, each taken with the lag that best fits the others; the version maintained today uses five, since NYSE turnover was dropped (trading has moved to high-frequency institutions and many venues). The proxies are the number of IPOs (sum over the past twelve months), their average first-day return, the closed-end fund discount, the equity share of new issues, and the dividend premium of Baker and Wurgler (2004). SENT⊥ first removes from each proxy its relation with industrial production, consumption, employment and recessions; in 2026 the authors changed this step to detrended rather than year-over-year macro series, to avoid extreme values around the COVID-19 recession.

The index is standardised, so its units say nothing about the size of shocks, and its components are smoothed or lagged by twelve months: the authors warn against using its changes. IPO data are from Ibbotson, Sindelar and Ritter (1994) and Jay Ritter’s updates; the fund discount from Neal and Wheatley (1998) and later sources (closed-end funds as a sentiment measure: Lee, Shleifer and Thaler, 1991); equity and debt issues from the Federal Reserve Bulletin. The current file is updated once a year; the dollar levels of equity and debt issuance come from the 2011 file and end in 2010.

Calibration: entry of new shares and the pricing of young firms

When firms exit and new firms enter, the value of entrants relative to the whole market, \(\varepsilon = I_0/p^A\) per quarter, pins down the relative cohort value \(\kappa = s/(1-\varepsilon)\) in the steady state. In the model, an entrant’s whole equity is new, so the data counterpart is the full value of the firms that go public, the market value of IPOs at their first closing price, not the cash they raise. Gross equity issues relative to market value measure something else, the cash raised by all issuers: IPO proceeds are only about a quarter of them (23 percent over 1980–2010), the rest being seasoned offerings of firms already listed, which the model’s entrants do not cover. The gross-issues series ends in 2010, when the Federal Reserve Bulletin data used by Baker and Wurgler stop, and its coverage (all corporations) is wider than that of the market value (nonfinancial corporations). It is kept for comparison.

Sentiment concerns how young, small and hard-to-value firms are priced against the rest of the market, so its persistence is a natural benchmark for shocks to the relative price of entrants; its limits, and how it maps to the model, are in Sentiment in the data and in the model.

The equity share of new issues of Baker and Wurgler, often written \(s\), is not the survival rate \(s\) of the site’s models; it is called “equity share of new issues” here.

Sentiment in the data and in the model

What the index can and cannot tell. Its persistence, about 0.93 per quarter for the orthogonalised index, supports a persistence of shocks to the pricing of young firms of the order of 0.95. Two caveats limit that use. First, the components are smoothed or lagged over twelve months (IPO numbers summed over the past year, first-day returns averaged over it, the equity share computed over it, the dividend premium lagged a year), which mechanically raises the persistence of the index above that of the sentiment it measures. Second, the index is a first principal component scaled to unit variance: its units have no economic meaning, so it says nothing about the size of the shocks. It can serve as an observable in estimation, where the model sets the scale, but not to calibrate the standard deviation of the shocks.

In reality. Sentiment is the demand of investors for speculative stocks, unrelated to fundamentals: when it is high, young, small, unprofitable, high-volatility and growth firms are priced high relative to old, large and dividend-paying ones, more firms go public, at higher first-day returns, and firms raise more of their funds with equity. Baker and Wurgler (2006) show that these stocks then earn low returns: the mispricing corrects.

In the model of stock-market dynamics. Firms survive each quarter with probability \(s\) and entrants sell new shares. The parameter \(\kappa\) is the price of a cohort of firms relative to the cohort one quarter older, \(p_{t,a-1} = \kappa_t\, p_{t,a}\), with \(s < \kappa < 1\): the higher \(\kappa\), the closer young firms are priced to old ones. A positive shock to \(\kappa_t\), a log AR(1) with persistence \(\rho_\kappa\), is the model’s counterpart of a rise in sentiment: young firms are worth more relative to the market, and the steady-state share of new issues in market value, \(1 - s/\kappa\), rises with \(\kappa\), as IPOs and equity issues rise with sentiment in the data. Literally, then, \(\kappa\) is a relative valuation of cohorts, not the sentiment index itself: the index is an ordinal, standardised measure of the same relative pricing. Two differences remain. In the model, the value of young firms reflects valuation, not mispricing that later reverses unless \(\kappa\) returns to its mean; and entrants issue at the market price, so the first-day return of IPOs (about 17 percent) has no counterpart, and gross issues include the new shares of existing firms, which the model’s entrants do not cover.

IPO data and the value of entrants

Data. Jay Ritter’s Initial Public Offerings: Updated Statistics (University of Florida), Tables 1 and 1a, give by year since 1980 the number of IPOs, their first-day returns, the proceeds and the aggregate market value at the first closing price, using all share classes and the number of shares after the offering, in current dollars. His sample is operating companies listed on the NYSE, Nasdaq or Amex: offers at a price of at least $5, without ADRs, unit offers, SPACs, closed-end funds, REITs, natural-resource partnerships, banks and savings institutions, small best-efforts offers and stocks not in CRSP. Ritter updates the tables once or twice a year and sometimes revises past years; the page reads his file at each update and keeps the last version read if it cannot.

The ratio. \(\varepsilon\) for year \(t\) is the market value of the year’s IPOs over the market value of equities at the end of year \(t-1\), converted to a rate per quarter, \(1-(1-x)^{1/4}\), and averaged. Both are nominal, so no price index is needed. Three denominators are shown. The Federal Reserve’s Financial Accounts (Z.1, table L.224) value the equities of nonfinancial corporations as public plus closely held corporations: the total includes firms that are not listed and will never go public, so it is a broad denominator that makes \(\varepsilon\) small. The Z.1 public corporate equities exclude closely held corporations from 1996 Q4 only. The market capitalisation of listed domestic companies (World Federation of Exchanges, via the World Bank) covers the listed firms that IPOs join, financial companies included.

First close or offer price. IPOs are underpriced: the price rises on the first day by about 19 percent on average (Ritter). Valued at the offer price, the market value of IPOs is the first-close value divided by one plus the first-day return; with the proceeds-weighted return, closer to a value weighting, this gives a lower bound for \(\varepsilon\). In the model, entrants issue at the market price, so the first-close value is the closer counterpart.

Which value of entrants. IPOs are the listed firms’ route to entry; most new firms never list, and many firms list years after they are born. The Business Dynamics page gives the other measure, for all firms: the employment share of startups (Value of entrants), from the same data as the survival rate \(s\).
