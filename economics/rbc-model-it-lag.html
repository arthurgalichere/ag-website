RBC Model: Firms Facing an Investment Installation Lag

This model extends the canonical RBC model in two ways. Firms, rather than households, own the capital stock: households hold shares in the firm, and the firm invests out of its revenue and pays the rest as dividends. And investment takes time to become productive: only a fraction \(\phi_t\) of the investment made in period \(t\) is installed in \(t+1\); the rest is installed one period later. The speed of installation is itself subject to shocks, so the economy is driven by two sources of fluctuations: technology shocks and installation shocks. The model is calibrated as the canonical one, simulated with the same technology shocks measured in the US data, and compared with both the data and the canonical model.

The Model

Households

The household’s problem

A representative household consumes \(c_t\) and divides one unit of time between leisure, \(l_t\), and hours of work, \(h_t\), so that \(l_t + h_t = 1\). It saves by holding shares of the representative firm, \(S_t\), bought at the price \(p_t\) and paying the dividend \(d_t\):

\[ \max_{\{c_t,\, h_t,\, S_t\}_{t=0}^{\infty}}\; \mathbb{E}_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t \left[ \ln c_t + \theta \ln (1-h_t) \right] \] \[ \text{subject to} \quad c_t + p_t S_t = (p_t + d_t)\, S_{t-1} + w_t h_t , \qquad S_{-1} \ \text{given} . \]

With \(\lambda_t\) the multiplier on the budget constraint, the first-order conditions give \(\lambda_t = 1/c_t\), labour supply and the Euler equation for shares:

\[ \frac{\theta}{1-h_t} = \frac{w_t}{c_t} , \qquad \frac{1}{c_t} = \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{1}{c_{t+1}}\, \frac{p_{t+1} + d_{t+1}}{p_t} \right] , \]

where \((p_{t+1} + d_{t+1})/p_t\) is the gross return on holding the firm’s stock.

Firms

Production, investment and the firm’s problem

A representative firm produces with hours and its own capital stock. As in the canonical model, productivity has a stationary part, \(A_t\), which follows a first-order autoregressive process, and labour-augmenting technical progress, \(X_t\), which grows at the constant rate \(g_X\) and gives the economy a balanced growth path:

\[ Y_t = A_t\, K_t^{\alpha} \left( X_t h_t \right)^{1-\alpha} , \qquad \ln A_t = \rho_A \ln A_{t-1} + \varepsilon^A_t , \qquad \varepsilon^A_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\varepsilon^A}) , \qquad X_{t+1} = (1+g_X)\, X_t . \]

It pays wages and invests out of its revenue, and distributes the rest as dividends. Investment takes time to install: a fraction \(\phi_t\) of the investment made in period \(t\) becomes productive capital in \(t+1\), and the remaining fraction \(1-\phi_t\) one period later. The speed of installation fluctuates around its steady-state value, \(\phi \in (0,1]\), with installation shocks \(\varepsilon^\phi_t\):

\[ d_t + w_t h_t + I_t = Y_t , \qquad K_{t+1} = (1-\delta)\, K_t + \phi_t I_t + (1-\phi_{t-1})\, I_{t-1} , \] \[ \ln \phi_t = (1-\rho_\phi) \ln \phi + \rho_\phi \ln \phi_{t-1} + \varepsilon^\phi_t , \qquad \varepsilon^\phi_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\varepsilon^\phi}) . \]

The firm maximises its value to its shareholders, the expected discounted stream of dividends, using their stochastic discount factor, \(\beta^t \lambda_t / \lambda_0\), by choosing hours, investment and next period’s capital, given \(K_0\) and the investment still to be installed from the previous period, \((1-\phi_{-1})\, I_{-1}\):

\[ \max_{\{d_t,\, h_t,\, I_t,\, K_{t+1}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}}\; \mathbb{E}_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t\, \frac{\lambda_t}{\lambda_0}\, d_t \]

subject to the flow-of-funds constraint and the accumulation of capital. With \(q_t\), the multiplier on the accumulation of capital, measuring the value of a unit of installed capital (Tobin’s \(q\)), and an interior solution for investment, the first-order conditions are

\[ w_t = (1-\alpha)\, \frac{Y_t}{h_t} , \qquad 1 = \phi_t\, q_t + \beta\, (1-\phi_t)\, \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t}\, q_{t+1} \right] , \] \[ q_t = \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t} \left( \alpha\, \frac{Y_{t+1}}{K_{t+1}} + (1-\delta)\, q_{t+1} \right) \right] . \]

A unit of output invested today costs one unit and buys \(\phi_t\) units of capital installed next period, worth \(q_t\) each, and \(1-\phi_t\) units installed a period later, worth \(q_{t+1}\) each, discounted by the shareholders. Installed capital is worth its marginal product next period plus its undepreciated value.

General Equilibrium

Definition of competitive equilibrium

Given the initial capital stock, \(K_0\), the investment made in the previous period and its installed fraction, \(I_{-1}\) and \(\phi_{-1}\), the household’s initial portfolio, \(S_{-1}\), the initial levels of technology and of the speed of installation, \(A_0\) and \(\phi_0\), and their stochastic processes, a competitive equilibrium consists of state-contingent sequences of household choices (consumption, labour supply and share holdings), firm choices (labour demand, investment, capital and dividends) and prices (the wage and the share price), in which every variable dated \(t\) depends only on the history of shocks up to \(t\), such that:

  1. Household optimisation. Given prices, households maximise lifetime expected utility subject to their sequence of budget constraints.
  2. Firm optimisation. Given prices and the shareholders’ stochastic discount factor, the firm maximises the expected discounted value of its dividends subject to its flow-of-funds constraint and the accumulation of capital.
  3. Market clearing. In every period and for every history of shocks, the labour market clears, the household holds the whole stock of the firm, \(S_t = 1\), and the goods market clears: combining the two budget constraints, \(c_t + I_t = Y_t\).

Steady State

The steady state

Installation lags raise the value of capital

In the steady state, \(A = 1\) and \(\phi_t = \phi\), so the accumulation of capital gives \(I = \delta K\), and the first-order condition for investment gives the value of installed capital:

\[ q = \frac{1}{\phi + \beta\, (1-\phi)} . \]

With \(r \equiv 1/\beta - 1 + \delta\), the condition for capital fixes the capital–labour ratio, and with it the great ratios:

\[ \frac{K}{h} = \left( \frac{\alpha}{q\, r} \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} , \qquad \frac{Y}{K} = \frac{q\, r}{\alpha} , \qquad \frac{I}{K} = \delta , \qquad \frac{c}{K} = \frac{q\, r}{\alpha} - \delta , \qquad \frac{d}{K} = q\, r - \delta . \]

Labour supply then gives hours, and the share price is the present value of dividends:

\[ h = \frac{(1-\alpha)\, Y/c}{\theta + (1-\alpha)\, Y/c} , \quad \text{with} \quad \frac{Y}{c} = \frac{q\, r}{q\, r - \alpha \delta} , \qquad p = \frac{\beta}{1-\beta}\, d . \]

These expressions are for an economy without trend growth. With trend growth, as in the simulations, \(\beta\) is replaced by \(\beta/(1+g_X)\) in \(q\), \(r\) becomes \((1+g_X)/\beta - 1 + \delta\), and the investment–capital ratio is \(I/K = (g_X + \delta)/[\phi + (1-\phi)/(1+g_X)]\).

When \(\phi = 1\), all investment is installed within one period, \(q = 1\), and the model is the standard RBC model with firm-owned capital, in which the marginal product of capital equals \(r\). When \(\phi < 1\), part of each unit of investment is installed a period later and its return is discounted by \(\beta\), so \(q > 1\): installed capital is worth more than the unit of output it costs. The marginal product of capital required rises to \(q\, r\), and the steady-state capital–labour ratio is lower than in the standard model.

Impulse Responses to One Positive Technology Shock

With an installation lag, most of each period’s investment becomes productive capital only two quarters later, so capital responds more slowly to the shock than in the canonical model, and the higher return on capital is reaped later. Investment, hours and output therefore rise less after the first quarter, while consumption, which depends on lifetime wealth, is almost unchanged.

Impulse Responses to One Positive Installation Shock

A positive shock of 1 percent to the speed of installation, \(\varepsilon^\phi_1\), hits the economy on its balanced growth path in quarter 1, with no further shocks: a larger share of this quarter’s investment is installed next quarter. One standard deviation of the shock is about a third of this size.

Faster installation makes investment pay off sooner, so firms invest more and the value of installed capital, Tobin’s q, falls towards its replacement cost: new capital is quicker to obtain. Capital rises the next quarter, and output with it, before both return slowly to their paths; households finance the extra investment by working more, which lowers the real wage at first, and by consuming less for a few quarters. With a steady-state speed of installation of 0.17, a 1 percent shock changes the share of investment installed within a quarter by less than 0.2 percentage points, so the responses are small.

Definitions, Methods and Solutions

Equilibrium conditions

The equilibrium is described by eight equations in the eight endogenous variables \(\lambda_t, c_t, h_t, p_t, d_t, q_t, K_{t+1}\) and \(I_t\), given the processes for technology, \(A_t\), and the speed of installation, \(\phi_t\):

\[ \frac{1}{c_t} = \lambda_t , \qquad \frac{\theta}{1-h_t} = \frac{(1-\alpha)\, A_t K_t^{\alpha} h_t^{-\alpha}}{c_t} , \qquad \frac{1}{c_t} = \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{1}{c_{t+1}}\, \frac{p_{t+1} + d_{t+1}}{p_t} \right] , \]
\[ 1 = \phi_t\, q_t + \beta\, (1-\phi_t)\, \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t}\, q_{t+1} \right] , \qquad q_t = \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t} \left( \alpha A_{t+1} K_{t+1}^{\alpha-1} h_{t+1}^{1-\alpha} + (1-\delta)\, q_{t+1} \right) \right] , \]
\[ d_t = \alpha\, A_t K_t^{\alpha} h_t^{1-\alpha} - I_t , \qquad c_t + I_t = A_t K_t^{\alpha} h_t^{1-\alpha} , \qquad K_{t+1} = (1-\delta)\, K_t + \phi_t I_t + (1-\phi_{t-1})\, I_{t-1} . \]

The first three describe the household (the multiplier, labour supply with the wage substituted out, and the Euler equation for shares), the next two the firm’s investment and capital decisions, and the last three the firm’s dividends, the resource constraint of the economy and the accumulation of capital. Because part of each period’s investment is installed a period later, last period’s investment, \(I_{t-1}\), is a state variable alongside capital.

Solution

The equilibrium conditions are written for the stationary economy, with growing variables divided by \(X_t\), and investment awaiting installation, \(z_t = (1-\phi_{t-1})\, I_{t-1}\), as a state variable. They are linearised in logarithms around the steady state and solved by the generalised Schur (QZ) decomposition of Klein (2000), which handles the static conditions and the two lags in the accumulation of capital directly. The model has four predetermined states, technology, the speed of installation, capital and investment awaiting installation, and a unique stable solution when four eigenvalues lie inside the unit circle, as the table reports. With \(\phi = 1\), the same solver reproduces the solution of the canonical model.
