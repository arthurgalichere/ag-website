Debt and Leverage

How much the US economy borrows, and who borrows: the debt of households, nonfinancial businesses and governments since 1950, in real terms and relative to GDP, the balance sheet and leverage of nonfinancial corporations, and the credit-to-GDP gap that regulators watch as an early warning of financial crises.

Debt by sector

Debt securities and loans owed by each sector, from the Financial Accounts of the United States, deflated by the GDP price index (2017 prices). The domestic nonfinancial sectors are households and nonprofit organisations, nonfinancial businesses and governments.

Debt relative to GDP

Debt at the end of each quarter as a percentage of nominal GDP at an annual rate, the usual measure of indebtedness. Rapid growth of private debt relative to GDP has preceded deep recessions and financial crises (Schularick and Taylor, 2012; Jordà, Schularick and Taylor, 2013), and high household debt deepened the Great Recession (Mian and Sufi, 2014).

Corporate balance sheet and leverage

The balance sheet of nonfinancial corporations links their debt to what they own and to what their shareholders own: total assets equal total liabilities, of which debt is the largest part, plus net worth. Leverage scales debt by one of these, or by the value the stock market puts on the firms, and each scale tells a different story.

Total assets
Everything the corporations own: financial assets, and real assets (structures, equipment, intellectual property, inventories, land) valued at current cost.
Debt
Debt securities (bonds, commercial paper) and loans, the interest-bearing liabilities. The other liabilities are mainly trade payables, taxes payable and foreign direct investment.
Net worth
Total assets minus total liabilities: what the firms are worth to their owners on the balance sheet, at market value (real assets at current cost) or at historical cost (at their original prices, a book value).
Market value of equities
What the corporations’ shares are worth on the stock market.
Tobin’s q
The market value of equities relative to net worth at market value. Above one, the stock market values the firms above the replacement cost of their assets (Tobin, 1969).

How the leverage measures relate to one another, and why they matter

Credit to the private nonfinancial sector

The Bank for International Settlements measures credit to households and nonfinancial corporations from all lenders (banks, bond markets and others), as a percentage of GDP, on a basis comparable across countries. Its gap to a slow-moving trend is the guide to the countercyclical capital buffer of Basel III.

Cyclical components

Debt adjusts slowly, so its cyclical components are smooth; government debt rises in recessions, as tax revenues fall and spending rises. Each quarterly series is filtered on its full history; each chart compares its cyclical component with that of output (real GDP), drawn in red. The statistics use the quarters each variable shares with output, from 1950 Q1 or its first quarter.

Definitions, sources and methods

Financial Accounts debt

Debt is debt securities and loans, the liabilities that bear interest, at the end of each quarter, from the Federal Reserve’s Financial Accounts of the United States (Z.1, table D.3): domestic nonfinancial sectors (TCMDODNS), households and nonprofit organisations (CMDEBT), nonfinancial business (TBSDODNS), of which nonfinancial corporate business (BCNSDODNS), the federal government (FGSDODNS) and state and local governments (SLGSDODNS); all sectors (TCMDO) adds the financial sector and the rest of the world. Government debt is the sum of federal, state and local debt. Federal debt includes Treasury securities held by the public but not those held by government trust funds.

Real debt divides the nominal level by the GDP price index (GDPDEF, 2017 = 100); this is the author’s construction, not an official real series. Debt-to-GDP ratios divide it by nominal GDP at a seasonally adjusted annual rate (GDP). Before 1951 Q4 the Financial Accounts give year-end values only; the other quarters are interpolated (see Missing data and estimates).

The corporate balance sheet and leverage measures are described in the next ribbon.

Corporate balance sheet and leverage measures

The balance sheet. The Financial Accounts’ balance sheet of nonfinancial corporate business (table B.103) gives total assets (TABSNNCB), total liabilities (TLBSNNCB) and net worth at market value (TNWMVBSNNCB), with \(\text{assets} = \text{liabilities} + \text{net worth}\). Real assets are valued at current (replacement) cost, financial assets at market value where available. Debt (BCNSDODNS) is the interest-bearing part of liabilities, about half of them.

How the measures relate. With \(D\) debt, \(A\) total assets, \(L\) total liabilities, \(NW\) net worth and \(E\) the market value of equities, the measures on this page are \(D/A\), \(D/NW\) (market value and historical cost) and \(D/E\). They are linked by \(D/NW = (D/A)\,/\,(1 - L/A)\) and \(D/E = (D/NW)\,/\,q\), where \(q = E/NW\) is Tobin’s q. The first two are balance-sheet measures, which move slowly; the third moves with the stock market, so most of its swings are swings in \(q\). Debt to total assets is the author’s calculation (BCNSDODNS / TABSNNCB); the other ratios are published by the Financial Accounts (NCBCMDPNWMV, NCBCMDPNWHC, NCBCMDPMVCE), and q is the ratio of two of them, NCBCMDPNWMV / NCBCMDPMVCE.

Why they matter. Leverage makes shareholders bear more risk: with more debt, the expected return on equity rises (Modigliani and Miller, 1958, Proposition II), and firms with high debt-to-equity ratios have earned higher stock returns (Bhandari, 1988). A fall in stock prices raises leverage at market value, one explanation of why stock-market volatility rises after falls (the leverage effect; Black, 1976). In macroeconomic models with financial frictions, firms’ net worth determines how much they can borrow, so falls in asset prices and net worth amplify recessions (Kiyotaki and Moore, 1997; Bernanke, Gertler and Gilchrist, 1999). Tobin’s q links the stock market to investment: firms invest more when the market values their capital above its replacement cost (Tobin, 1969; Hayashi, 1982). Over the long run, US corporate leverage has risen markedly since the Second World War (Graham, Leary and Roberts, 2015).

Caution. Net worth at historical cost values assets at their original prices, so it depends on past inflation and on accounting conventions; the series rises to implausibly high values between the mid-1950s and about 1970, as published, while the market-value series does not.

BIS credit and the credit-to-GDP gap

The Bank for International Settlements’ total credit statistics measure credit to the private nonfinancial sector (households and nonprofit institutions serving households, and nonfinancial corporations) from all sources, at market value, adjusted for breaks, as a percentage of GDP (QUSPAM770A, QUSHAM770A, QUSNAM770A; Dembiermont, Drehmann and Muksakunratana, 2013). They are released with a lag of about two quarters.

The credit-to-GDP gap is the ratio minus its trend from a one-sided Hodrick–Prescott filter with λ = 400,000, the Basel Committee’s method: at each quarter, the filter is run on the data up to that quarter only, and its last value is the trend (Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, 2010). The high smoothing parameter matches credit cycles, about four times as long as business cycles (Drehmann, Borio and Tsatsaronis, 2011). The trend starts after ten years of data. This is the author’s calculation; the BIS publishes its own gap, which can differ slightly in its starting point.

Cyclical components and statistics

Real debt and Tobin’s q are filtered as \(100 \times \ln x_t\), so their cycles are percent deviations from trend; debt and credit ratios and leverage are filtered in levels (percentage points). The filters are those of the Real Business Cycles page: Hodrick–Prescott (λ = 1600), Hamilton (2018; h = 8, p = 4) and Christiano–Fitzgerald (2003; 6–32 quarters), each applied to the full history and shown from 1950.

Missing data and estimates

Where a source did not publish a value for a period, the value is estimated and flagged rather than left blank, because gaps break time-series and econometric analysis. An isolated gap of up to three consecutive periods is filled by linear interpolation between the published values on either side, in logs for series measured in levels (constant growth across the gap). Estimates in an input also flag the measures built from it. The downloads mark them in an _estimated column (1 = estimated).
