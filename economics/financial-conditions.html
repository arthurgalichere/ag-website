Financial Conditions and Stress

How easy or hard it is to borrow and how much risk investors will bear: composite indices of financial conditions and stress, stock-market volatility, corporate credit spreads, the excess bond premium and the slope of the yield curve — indicators that tend to deteriorate before and during recessions.

Financial conditions indices

Composite indices summarise many market and balance-sheet indicators into one number, scaled so that zero is the average (or normal) level and positive values mean tighter conditions or more stress than usual. Tight financial conditions signal a higher risk of weak growth ahead (Adrian, Boyarchenko and Giannone, 2019). Weekly and monthly values are averaged over each quarter.

Volatility and credit spreads

Spreads are the extra yield that investors demand to hold riskier debt; volatility measures the uncertainty priced in stock options. Both rise when risk appetite falls.

Cyclical components

Financial stress, volatility and credit spreads are countercyclical: they rise as output falls below trend. Each quarterly series is filtered on its full history; each chart compares its cyclical component with that of output (real GDP), drawn in red. The statistics use the quarters each variable shares with output, from 1950 Q1 or its first quarter.

Definitions, sources and methods

Data
  • Chicago Fed NFCI, ANFCI and subindices (weekly, from January 1971; NFCI, ANFCI, NFCIRISK, NFCICREDIT, NFCILEVERAGE, NFCINONFINLEVERAGE). The indices are re-estimated every week, so their whole history can be revised.
  • St. Louis Fed Financial Stress Index (weekly, from December 1993; STLFSI4) and Kansas City Financial Stress Index (monthly, from February 1990; KCFSI).
  • VIX (daily, from January 1990; VIXCLS), Chicago Board Options Exchange.
  • Corporate bond yields: Moody’s seasoned Aaa and Baa yields (AAA, BAA), and the 10-year Treasury constant-maturity yield (GS10, from April 1953) and 3-month Treasury bill rate (TB3MS), monthly averages (Federal Reserve H.15).
  • GZ credit spread and excess bond premium (monthly, from January 1973), as updated by the Federal Reserve Board (Favara, Gilchrist, Lewis and Zakrajšek, 2016).

Weekly and daily series are averaged over each complete month, and monthly values over each complete quarter. Spreads are the author’s calculations: the difference between the two monthly average yields, in percentage points.

Cyclical components and statistics

The quarterly series are filtered in levels, so their cycles are in index points or percentage points. The filters are those of the Real Business Cycles page: Hodrick–Prescott (λ = 1600), Hamilton (2018; h = 8, p = 4) and Christiano–Fitzgerald (2003; 6–32 quarters). The indices are already stationary by construction; filtering them removes slow movements and keeps their business-cycle component, comparable with that of output.

Missing data and estimates

Where a source did not publish a value for a period, the value is estimated and flagged rather than left blank, because gaps break time-series and econometric analysis. An isolated gap of up to three consecutive periods is filled by linear interpolation between the published values on either side, in logs for series measured in levels (constant growth across the gap). Estimates in an input also flag the measures built from it. The downloads mark them in an _estimated column (1 = estimated).
