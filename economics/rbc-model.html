RBC Model Simulation and Forecasting

The Real Business Cycle model explains fluctuations as the efficient response of households and firms to changes in technology. Households choose how much to consume, save and work; firms produce with capital and labour; and the only source of fluctuations is technology. This page solves the basic model of King and Rebelo (1999), feeds it with the technology shocks measured in the US data, and compares the business cycles the model generates since 1950 with those observed.

Definitions and methods

The model: households, firms and balanced growth

The model is the basic neoclassical model of King and Rebelo (1999, sections 3 and 4), in the tradition of Kydland and Prescott (1982), Long and Plosser (1983) and Prescott (1986). A representative household values consumption, \(C_t\), and leisure, \(1-N_t\), where \(N_t\) is the share of its time spent working:

\[ \max\; E_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t \left[ \ln C_t + \xi \ln (1-N_t) \right] . \]

Firms produce with capital, \(K_t\), and hours, using a Cobb–Douglas production function with two forms of technology: \(A_t\), which fluctuates around its trend and drives the business cycle, and \(X_t\), labour-augmenting technical progress that grows at the constant gross rate \(\gamma\) each quarter:

\[ Y_t = A_t K_t^{\alpha} (X_t N_t)^{1-\alpha}, \qquad X_{t+1} = \gamma X_t . \]

Output is consumed or invested, and capital depreciates at the rate \(\delta\):

\[ C_t + I_t = Y_t, \qquad K_{t+1} = (1-\delta) K_t + I_t . \]

Balanced growth. Because trend growth is labour-augmenting (Harrod-neutral) and utility is logarithmic in consumption, the economy grows along a balanced growth path (King, Plosser and Rebelo, 1988): output, consumption, investment, capital and the real wage all grow at the rate of \(X_t\), while hours and the interest rate are constant. Dividing each growing variable by \(X_t\), as in \(y_t = Y_t/X_t\) and \(k_t = K_t/X_t\), gives a stationary economy, which has a steady state. Its conditions are those of the economy without growth, except that the growth of \(X_t\) appears in the accumulation of capital and in the discount factor:

\[ c_t + \gamma\, k_{t+1} = A_t k_t^{\alpha} N_t^{1-\alpha} + (1-\delta) k_t , \]
\[ \frac{\xi\, c_t}{1-N_t} = (1-\alpha) \frac{y_t}{N_t} , \qquad \frac{1}{c_t} = \frac{\beta}{\gamma}\, E_t \left[ \frac{1}{c_{t+1}} \left( \alpha \frac{y_{t+1}}{k_{t+1}} + 1 - \delta \right) \right] . \]

The first is the resource constraint, the second equates the value of leisure to the real wage, and the third is the Euler equation for saving. In the steady state, the marginal product of capital is \(\alpha\, y/k = \gamma/\beta - 1 + \delta\): faster growth raises the interest rate and lowers the capital–output ratio. The model’s series are percent deviations from the balanced growth path, so that, in logarithms, \(\ln Y_t = \ln y + \ln X_t + \hat{y}_t\): a constant, a linear trend and the cycle. The HP filter removes a linear trend exactly, so trend growth affects the model’s cycles only through the steady state and the dynamics, not through the trend itself.

Technology. With Hicks-neutral technology, \(A_t\) is total factor productivity, as in King and Rebelo. With Harrod-neutral technology, \(Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} (Z_t X_t N_t)^{1-\alpha}\), so that \(A_t = Z_t^{1-\alpha}\) (see “Forms of technical progress” below). In both cases the trend of technology estimated in the data fixes \(\gamma\): the trend growth of TFP is \(1-\alpha\) times the growth of \(X_t\).

Solving and simulating the model

Log-linearisation. Each condition is approximated around the steady state in percent deviations, written with a hat (\(\hat{y}_t = 100 \ln (y_t/y)\)). With \(\Upsilon = \alpha + N/(1-N)\) and \(\Omega = 1 - \beta(1-\delta)/\gamma\):

\[ \hat{y}_t = \hat{A}_t + \alpha\, \hat{k}_t + (1-\alpha)\, \hat{N}_t , \qquad \hat{N}_t = \frac{\hat{A}_t + \alpha\, \hat{k}_t - \hat{c}_t}{\Upsilon} , \]
\[ \gamma\, \frac{k}{y}\, \hat{k}_{t+1} = \hat{y}_t + (1-\delta) \frac{k}{y}\, \hat{k}_t - \frac{c}{y}\, \hat{c}_t , \qquad E_t \hat{c}_{t+1} - \hat{c}_t = \Omega\, E_t \left( \hat{y}_{t+1} - \hat{k}_{t+1} \right) , \]
\[ \hat{A}_{t+1} = \rho\, \hat{A}_t + \varepsilon^A_{t+1} . \]

Eliminating hours with the labour-supply condition leaves three equations in technology and capital, which are predetermined, and consumption, which is free to jump.

Solution. The system is solved by the eigenvalue decomposition of Blanchard and Kahn (1980). It has a unique stable solution when the number of eigenvalues outside the unit circle equals the number of jump variables, here one; the table above reports the eigenvalues. Consumption then follows the saddle path

\[ \hat{c}_t = g_A\, \hat{A}_t + g_K\, \hat{k}_t , \]

and every other variable follows from technology, capital and consumption: hours from the labour-supply condition, output from the production function, investment from the resource constraint, labour productivity and the real wage from \(\hat{y}_t - \hat{N}_t\) (the two are equal with a Cobb–Douglas production function), and the real interest rate from the Euler equation, \(\hat{r}_t = 4\,(E_t \hat{c}_{t+1} - \hat{c}_t)\), in percentage points a year.

Simulation. The model starts in the first quarter of the technology data (1947 Q1), with technology at its measured deviation from trend and capital at its steady state, and is then driven by the measured shocks, quarter by quarter. The first three years are not shown, so that the starting value of capital has little effect on the series shown.

Two ways of comparing the model with the data. The model’s series are deviations from the balanced growth path, while the data’s are HP cyclical components, from which slow movements around the trend have also been removed. King and Rebelo (1999, section 4.3.1) therefore HP filter the model’s series too (λ = 1600) before comparing them with the data: this is the “Linear trend” setting. The “HP-filtered” setting uses the shortcut described on the Extracting Technology Shocks page: the model is driven by the HP-filtered shocks, and its series are already HP cyclical components. Because the model is linear, the two coincide except near the ends of the sample; the comparison setting shows both.

The data. As in King and Rebelo, the model is compared with per-capita data: output, consumption and investment per person, hours per person aged 16 and over (nonfarm business sector), and Fernald’s capital input of the business sector per person. The real wage is real hourly compensation and labour productivity is output per hour, both in the nonfarm business sector. The real interest rate is the ex-post real three-month Treasury-bill rate; King and Rebelo use an expected real rate built from inflation forecasts (Stock and Watson, 1999).

Technology shocks, step by step

Following King and Rebelo (1999, section 4.1), the logarithm of total factor productivity is split into a linear trend and a deviation from that trend. The deviation is regressed on its value one quarter earlier, which gives its persistence, \(\rho\); the technology shock is the part of the deviation that last quarter does not predict, and its standard deviation, \(\sigma\), measures the size of the shocks. The model’s technology then follows

\[ \hat{A}_{t+1} = \rho\, \hat{A}_t + \varepsilon^A_{t+1} , \]

where \(\hat{A}_t\) is the percent deviation of technology from its trend and \(\varepsilon^A_t\) the technology shock (\(\varepsilon^{A^u}_t\) for utilization-adjusted TFP).

Forms of technical progress: Hicks, Harrod and Solow

Technical progress can enter the production function \(Y = F(K, N)\) in different places. The classic definitions say which ratio it leaves unchanged; they are called “neutral” because they do not change the distribution of income between capital and labour at a given value of that ratio.

  • Hicks-neutral (Hicks, 1932): \(Y = A\,F(K, N)\). Technology multiplies the whole production function, raising the marginal products of capital and labour in the same proportion, so the ratio of marginal products is unchanged at a given capital–labour ratio. \(A\) is total factor productivity; King and Rebelo (1999) use it for the technology shocks.
  • Harrod-neutral or labour-augmenting (Harrod, 1948): \(Y = F(K, Z N)\). Technology makes each hour of work more productive, as if there were more workers; it leaves the capital–output ratio unchanged at a given interest rate. It is the only form consistent with balanced growth for a general production function (Uzawa, 1961), which is why growth models, and the trend in King and Rebelo’s model, use it.
  • Solow-neutral or capital-augmenting: \(Y = F(B K, N)\). Technology makes each machine more productive, as if there were more capital; it leaves the labour–output ratio unchanged at a given wage.

Which form of technical progress should a model use? The long-run evidence favours Harrod neutrality. Kaldor (1961) summarised the facts of growth: output per worker grows at a roughly constant rate, while the capital–output ratio, the real return on capital and the shares of capital and labour in income stay roughly constant. These “stylised facts” describe balanced growth, and Uzawa (1961) showed that, for a general production function, balanced growth is possible only if technical progress is labour-augmenting. Harrod neutrality is therefore the natural modelling assumption, and the one used for trend growth in most growth and business-cycle models, including King and Rebelo’s. Kaldor (1957) himself went further and rejected the separation between moving along a production function and shifting it, replacing it with a “technical progress function” linking the growth of output per worker to the growth of capital per worker.

With a Cobb–Douglas production function, however, the choice does not matter for the data: the three forms are equivalent. If \(Y = A K^{\alpha} N^{1-\alpha}\), with \(\alpha\) the capital share, then

\[ A K^{\alpha} N^{1-\alpha} = K^{\alpha} (Z N)^{1-\alpha} = (B K)^{\alpha} N^{1-\alpha} \quad\text{with}\quad A = Z^{1-\alpha} = B^{\alpha} . \]

The data cannot tell them apart, but the size of the shocks depends on where technology is placed: in logarithms, \(\Delta \ln Z = \Delta \ln A / (1-\alpha)\), so labour-augmenting shocks are about one and a half times as large as TFP shocks, with the same persistence. The switch above the technology chart shows the Hicks- and Harrod-neutral versions; the one to use is the one that matches where technology enters the model’s production function.
