Technology shocks

Definitions and methods

Technology shocks, step by step

Following King and Rebelo (1999, section 4.1), the logarithm of total factor productivity is split into a linear trend and a deviation from that trend. The deviation is regressed on its value one quarter earlier, which gives its persistence, \(\rho\); the technology shock is the part of the deviation that last quarter does not predict, and its standard deviation, \(\sigma\), measures the size of the shocks. The model’s technology then follows

\[ \hat{A}_{t+1} = \rho\, \hat{A}_t + \varepsilon^A_{t+1} , \]

where \(\hat{A}_t\) is the percent deviation of technology from its trend and \(\varepsilon^A_t\) the technology shock (\(\varepsilon^{A^u}_t\) for utilization-adjusted TFP).

Forms of technical progress: Hicks, Harrod and Solow

Technical progress can enter the production function \(Y = F(K, N)\) in different places. The classic definitions say which ratio it leaves unchanged; they are called “neutral” because they do not change the distribution of income between capital and labour at a given value of that ratio.

  • Hicks-neutral (Hicks, 1932): \(Y = A\,F(K, N)\). Technology multiplies the whole production function, raising the marginal products of capital and labour in the same proportion, so the ratio of marginal products is unchanged at a given capital–labour ratio. \(A\) is total factor productivity; King and Rebelo (1999) use it for the technology shocks.
  • Harrod-neutral or labour-augmenting (Harrod, 1948): \(Y = F(K, Z N)\). Technology makes each hour of work more productive, as if there were more workers; it leaves the capital–output ratio unchanged at a given interest rate. It is the only form consistent with balanced growth for a general production function (Uzawa, 1961), which is why growth models, and the trend in King and Rebelo’s model, use it.
  • Solow-neutral or capital-augmenting: \(Y = F(B K, N)\). Technology makes each machine more productive, as if there were more capital; it leaves the labour–output ratio unchanged at a given wage.

Which form of technical progress should a model use? The long-run evidence favours Harrod neutrality. Kaldor (1961) summarised the facts of growth: output per worker grows at a roughly constant rate, while the capital–output ratio, the real return on capital and the shares of capital and labour in income stay roughly constant. These “stylised facts” describe balanced growth, and Uzawa (1961) showed that, for a general production function, balanced growth is possible only if technical progress is labour-augmenting. Harrod neutrality is therefore the natural modelling assumption, and the one used for trend growth in most growth and business-cycle models, including King and Rebelo’s. Kaldor (1957) himself went further and rejected the separation between moving along a production function and shifting it, replacing it with a “technical progress function” linking the growth of output per worker to the growth of capital per worker.

With a Cobb–Douglas production function, however, the choice does not matter for the data: the three forms are equivalent. If \(Y = A K^{\alpha} N^{1-\alpha}\), with \(\alpha\) the capital share, then

\[ A K^{\alpha} N^{1-\alpha} = K^{\alpha} (Z N)^{1-\alpha} = (B K)^{\alpha} N^{1-\alpha} \quad\text{with}\quad A = Z^{1-\alpha} = B^{\alpha} . \]

The data cannot tell them apart, but the size of the shocks depends on where technology is placed: in logarithms, \(\Delta \ln Z = \Delta \ln A / (1-\alpha)\), so labour-augmenting shocks are about one and a half times as large as TFP shocks, with the same persistence. The switch above the technology chart shows the Hicks- and Harrod-neutral versions; the one to use is the one that matches where technology enters the model’s production function.
