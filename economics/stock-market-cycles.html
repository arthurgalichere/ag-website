Stock Market Cycles

Stock prices are far more volatile than output, and than the dividends they are a claim on (Shiller, 1981). This page measures how the US stock market moves over the business cycle: the cyclical components of stock prices, dividends, earnings and valuation ratios, their volatility and co-movement with output, their leads and lags, and their correlations with the variables of the Real Business Cycles page. The levels and growth rates of these series are on Main Stock Market Indicators.

Definitions and methods

The series
  • Stock prices, dividends and earnings (S&P 500): quarterly averages of Robert J. Shiller’s monthly data: the price of the S&P Composite index (the S&P 500 from 1957), and dividends and earnings over the past twelve months per share of the index. See Main Stock Market Indicators for their definitions.
  • Corporate dividends (NIPA): net dividends paid by US corporations in the national income and product accounts (FRED: DIVIDEND), the aggregate counterpart of the dividends of a model economy; per capita, divided by the population (B230RC0Q173SBEA).
  • Price–dividend ratio and CAPE: the S&P 500 price over twelve-month dividends, and the real price over the average of real earnings in the previous ten years (Campbell and Shiller, 1988).
  • Real values: nominal values divided by the GDP price index (GDPDEF, 2017 = 100). The ratios are the same in real and nominal terms.
  • Output and the macroeconomic variables: in real terms, the cycles published on the Real Business Cycles page, unchanged. In nominal terms, the current-dollar series (GDP, PCEC, GPDI, GCE, EXPGS, IMPGS; hourly compensation COMPNFB; nominal output per hour = OPHNFB × IPDNBS / 100, the nonfarm business price deflator), filtered from 1947 Q1 in the same way, total or per capita (BEA population).
Filters and statistics

Every series is filtered as \(100 \times \ln x_t\) on its quarterly values from 1947 Q1, the start of the series of the Real Business Cycles page, so that the stock cycles and the macroeconomic cycles are measured over the same history; a cycle value of 10 means the series is about 10 percent above its trend. The filters are those of Real Business Cycles: Hodrick–Prescott (λ = 1600; Hodrick and Prescott, 1997), Hamilton (2018; h = 8, p = 4) and Christiano–Fitzgerald (2003; 6–32 quarters).

The statistics use the quarters from 1950 Q1 that each pair of series covers: the standard deviation of the cycle, its ratio to that of output, the first-order autocorrelation and the correlation with output; the cross-correlations follow Kydland and Prescott (1990).
