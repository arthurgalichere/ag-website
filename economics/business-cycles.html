Real Business Cycles

Models of the business cycle aim to explain two features of aggregate fluctuations. The first is volatility: how much each variable moves over the cycle, compared with output. The second is co-movement: whether it rises and falls with output. A variable is procyclical if it moves with output, countercyclical if it moves against it, and acyclical if it shows no consistent relationship. These are regularities across many cycles, not features of a single recession or expansion. This page measures them for the United States from 1950, on the cyclical component of each variable: its deviation from trend.

Missing data and estimates

The cycles on this page are computed from the series published on the Main Economic Indicators page, after the same preparation: monthly series are averaged over complete quarters; real components of consumption and investment are built in chained 2017 dollars with BEA’s formula before 2007; per-capita measures divide by BEA’s population (hours by the population aged 16 and over).

When a source did not publish a value for a period (for example, the October 2025 household survey, not collected during the federal government shutdown), the value is estimated by interpolation between the published values on either side and flagged. Such estimates are rare and short, so their effect on cycles and statistics is negligible. The list of estimated values, with the method and reason for each, is in Missing data and estimates on the Main Economic Indicators page.

Definitions and methods

What the cycle measures

Each filter splits a series into a trend and a cyclical component. Series measured in levels (output, its components, capital, hours, wages, productivity, TFP, debt) and the unemployment rate are filtered as 100 × their natural logarithm, so a cycle value of 2 means the variable is roughly 2 percent above its trend. Other rates and ratios (inflation, participation, interest rates, net exports as a share of GDP) are filtered as they are, and their cycles are in percentage points.

The cycle is a statistical estimate, not an observed quantity. Different filters define the trend differently, so their cycles differ in size and timing; comparing them shows which regularities are robust to the method. Every series is filtered on its full published history; charts and statistics start in 1950 Q1.

Filters

Three filters estimate the trend, and therefore the cycle, in different ways. The filter switch at the top of the page applies to every chart and table; “Filter comparison” shows each chart’s first variable under all three.

Hodrick–Prescott (HP) filter. The HP trend is the smooth series that best fits the data while penalising changes in its growth rate (Hodrick and Prescott, 1997). The smoothing parameter is λ = 1600, the convention for quarterly data and the standard choice in the real-business-cycle literature (Kydland and Prescott, 1990; Cooley and Prescott, 1995). The filter is two-sided: each trend value uses past and future observations, so the latest values are revised as new data arrive.

Hamilton filter. Hamilton (2018) regresses each series on its four most recent values known two years earlier (h = 8, p = 4 for quarterly data). The cycle is the part of the series that could not be predicted from that information. The method uses only past data, the first 11 quarters of each series have no value, and its cycles are usually larger than HP cycles because they include two years of unpredictable changes.

Christiano–Fitzgerald (CF) band-pass filter. The CF filter keeps fluctuations that repeat every 6 to 32 quarters (1.5 to 8 years), the usual definition of business-cycle frequencies, and removes slower movements and shorter-lived noise (Christiano and Fitzgerald, 2003). This page uses the asymmetric full-sample version, which assumes each series behaves like a random walk with drift. Its most recent values rely on fewer observations and are revised as new data arrive.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate is filtered in logs, like output, so its cycle is a percent deviation from trend rather than a deviation in percentage points. This is the convention of the labour-market literature. Because unemployment is a small number, a deviation in percentage points understates how much it moves: a rise from 5 to 6 percent is 1 point, but a 20 percent increase in the rate.

Shimer (2005) measures unemployment, vacancies and their ratio (labour-market tightness) as log deviations from a very smooth HP trend (λ = 105) in quarterly US data from 1951. The standard deviation of unemployment is about 0.19 (19 percent of trend), roughly ten times that of labour productivity, and the vacancy–unemployment ratio is about twenty times as volatile as productivity. The standard search-and-matching model (Mortensen and Pissarides, 1994), calibrated in the usual way, generates only a small fraction of these fluctuations from productivity shocks: the “unemployment volatility puzzle”.

Hagedorn and Manovskii (2008) use the standard quarterly smoothing parameter, λ = 1600, which assigns more of the slow movement in unemployment to the trend; the standard deviation of log unemployment is then about 0.125 (12.5 percent). They argue that when the value of non-market activity and workers’ bargaining power are calibrated to the cost of posting vacancies and to the cyclicality of wages, the model is consistent with the data. Whether that calibration is plausible remains debated.

The HP cycle on this page uses λ = 1600, as for every other variable, so it is comparable to Hagedorn and Manovskii; the Hamilton and Christiano–Fitzgerald filters are checks. The sample is longer than in both papers and includes the Great Recession and the 2020 pandemic, when unemployment rose very sharply, which raises the standard deviations.

The unemployment gap, 100 × (u − u*)/u*, measures the distance from the Congressional Budget Office’s noncyclical rate (NROU) on the same relative scale, without a statistical filter.

Business-cycle statistics
  • Standard deviation: of the cyclical component, in percent of trend (percentage points for rates and ratios). It measures volatility.
  • Relative standard deviation: the standard deviation divided by that of output over the same quarters (and, for the components of consumption and investment, by that of their total).
  • First-order autocorrelation: the correlation of the cycle with its value one quarter earlier. It measures persistence.
  • Correlation with output: the contemporaneous correlation of the two cycles. It measures co-movement: positive for procyclical variables, negative for countercyclical ones, close to zero for acyclical ones.
  • Cross-correlations: corr(xt+k, yt) for k = −4, …, +4 quarters, as in Kydland and Prescott (1990) and Stock and Watson (1999). The peak is the k with the largest correlation in absolute value.

Annual or quarterly rates. Inflation and interest rates are annualised by default. The quarterly basis divides their cycles by four (inflation becomes 100 × Δln P, interest rates the rate earned over one quarter), the units of a quarterly model such as Smets and Wouters (2007). This changes their standard deviations and relative standard deviations by the same factor, but not their autocorrelations, correlations or leads and lags. See the Main Economic Indicators page for which basis to use in estimation.

Each statistic uses all quarters from 1950 Q1 for which both series exist (for example, the federal funds rate starts in 1954 Q3 and Fernald’s data end one quarter earlier than the national accounts). The 2020–21 pandemic quarters are included; they are large and raise the standard deviations of most variables.

Variables and data

The series are those of the Main Economic Indicators page, with the same sources, quarterly averages of monthly series and treatment of missing values; see its Definitions and methods.

  • Per capita: output, its components and capital services are divided by the BEA mid-period population; hours by the civilian noninstitutional population aged 16 and over. Rates, ratios, wages, productivity, TFP and debt are the same under both measures.
  • Net exports: real net exports as a percentage of real GDP, since net exports can be negative.
  • Unemployment gap: 100 × (u − u*)/u*, where u* is the Congressional Budget Office’s noncyclical rate of unemployment (NROU): the distance of the unemployment rate from that rate, in percent of it. It is already a deviation, so it is not filtered and is the same under every filter.
  • Capital and TFP: John Fernald’s capital input and TFP series for the business sector (Fernald, 2014).
  • Real interest rates: ex-post rates: the nominal rate minus GDP-deflator inflation over the following quarter.
