Definitions and methods

Series, units and frequencies

All series are shown at a quarterly frequency, the standard frequency for business-cycle analysis and for estimating macroeconomic models. Series published monthly (the unemployment rate, participation rate, population aged 16 and over, consumer price index and Treasury bill rate) are averaged over the three months of each complete quarter. National-accounts series are at seasonally adjusted annual rates.

Charts, the clean CSV and the MATLAB file start in 1950 Q1. Every calculation — estimates of missing values, trends, growth rates and shares — uses each series’ full published history, and the raw CSV keeps that full history.

Per-capita measures divide each real aggregate by the BEA mid-period population (all ages, B230RC0Q173SBEA) and are in dollars per person.

Hours, productivity and real hourly compensation cover the nonfarm business sector, as published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Real hourly compensation is deflated by consumer prices. The participation rate is the labour force as a percentage of the civilian noninstitutional population aged 16 and over.

Real components of consumption and investment

The data problem. BEA publishes the components of consumption (durable goods, nondurable goods, services) and of fixed investment (nonresidential, residential) in chained 2017 dollars only from 2007 onwards. Their chain-type quantity and price indexes, and their current-dollar values, are published from 1947.

Construction. BEA defines a chained-dollar series as its chain-type (Fisher) quantity index multiplied by its current-dollar value in the reference year, divided by 100 (BEA, 2023, chapter 4). The components shown here apply exactly this formula from 1947: Xt = Qt × V2017 / 100, where Qt is the quantity index (2017 = 100) and V2017 the 2017 current-dollar value, the average of the four quarterly values at annual rates. From 2007 the result reproduces BEA’s published chained-dollar series to within rounding (about 0.001 percent); applied to total consumption and total investment, which BEA publishes in chained dollars from 1947, it reproduces the published series over the whole period to within 0.01 percent. Every update checks this again and stops if the difference exceeds 0.05 percent. The real change in private inventories is published by BEA in chained dollars from 1947 and is used as published; it has no quantity index because it can be negative.

Why not deflate? Dividing a nominal series by its own price index gives almost the same numbers, but not exactly, because BEA’s quarterly chain-type price index and the implicit price deflator differ slightly (by up to 0.16 percent for residential investment since 2007). The quantity-index formula is the one BEA itself uses.

Units and interpretation. Chained dollars keep the series in billions of dollars at 2017 prices, so the components and the total can be shown on one chart. They are not additive, however: the components do not sum to the total, and the gap widens with the distance from 2017 because relative prices change (BEA, 2023; Whelan, 2002). Real growth rates are unaffected, as each chained-dollar series grows exactly like its quantity index. Relative sizes in chained dollars are only rough approximations, and only close to 2017; BEA recommends current-dollar shares to measure the relative importance of components (BEA, 2023; Landefeld, Moulton and Vojtech, 2003), which is why the share tables report both and the nominal shares should be read first.

Shares and growth rates

Nominal shares divide each current-dollar component by the current-dollar total (GDP, consumption or investment). They add up to 100 (for GDP, with imports entering negatively) and are the measure of relative importance recommended by BEA, because current-dollar values are the weights of the chain-type indexes (BEA, 2023; Landefeld, Moulton and Vojtech, 2003).

Growth rates are annualised quarter-on-quarter log changes, averaged over each period. Real growth uses the chained 2017-dollar series, so it is not affected by the non-additivity of chained components.

Real ratios divide each chained 2017-dollar component by chained 2017-dollar GDP. A nominal share equals the real ratio times a relative price, PCC / (PYY) = (PC/PY)(C/Y), so the two differ whenever relative prices move — most notably for investment and for durable goods, whose relative prices have fallen for decades. Chained components are not additive, so real ratios do not sum to 100 (the “Sum of components” row of the consumption and investment tables shows by how much) and are most reliable close to the 2017 reference year. Whelan (2002) shows that ratios of chain-aggregated real series can be seriously misleading far from the reference year and recommends nominal shares instead.

Inflation and real interest rates

Inflation is the annualised quarter-on-quarter change, 400 × [ln(Pt) − ln(Pt−1)], in percent. For the consumer price index (CPI, all urban consumers), the monthly index is first averaged over each complete quarter.

The two measures cover different baskets. The GDP deflator is the price of everything produced in the US — consumption, investment, government purchases and exports, but not imports — so it matches the output measure, real GDP. This is why it is the usual inflation measure when estimating macroeconomic models, which are often closed-economy (for example Smets and Wouters, 2007). The CPI is the price of a fixed basket of goods and services bought by urban households, including imported goods; it is the measure most people experience and the basis of many indexation rules.

The ex-post real Treasury bill rate is the quarterly average of the monthly nominal rate minus GDP-deflator inflation realised over the following quarter, both at annual rates and in percentage points. It measures the real return actually earned, not the rate expected when the bill was bought.

The real rate chart can be shown at an annual rate (default) or per quarter. The quarterly rate is the annual rate divided by four: the nominal rate earned over one quarter minus the quarterly log change in the GDP deflator, 100 × [ln(Pt+1) − ln(Pt)]. It is the unit used when comparing data with quarterly models.

Corporate debt

Debt securities and loans of nonfinancial corporate businesses (Federal Reserve Financial Accounts), divided by the GDP price deflator to express it in 2017 prices. This is the author’s construction, not an official real series. Before 1951 Q4 the source gives year-end values only; the other quarters are interpolated (see Missing data and estimates).

Trends

On charts with several series, each trend is drawn in its series’ colour (dashed for HP, dotted for Christiano–Fitzgerald). Trends are estimated on each series’ full history, before 1950 included.

Trends are statistical estimates, not observed data. For series measured in levels they are estimated on 100 × the natural logarithm and converted back to the series’ own units; for rates (inflation, unemployment, participation, interest rates) they are estimated on the rate itself.

  • HP: Hodrick–Prescott filter, λ = 1600 (quarterly). It is smooth and two-sided, so its latest values are revised as new data arrive.
  • Christiano–Fitzgerald: the series minus its business-cycle component, defined as fluctuations lasting 6–32 quarters. It still contains fluctuations shorter than the band, so it is less smooth than the HP trend; its latest values are also revised.

Unemployment rate: instead of a statistical trend, the chart shows the Congressional Budget Office’s noncyclical rate of unemployment (NROU), the standard US estimate of the natural rate. Statistical filters applied to the unemployment rate tend to follow its large cyclical swings, whereas the unemployment gap relative to the NROU is the usual measure of labour-market slack (for example in Okun’s law, Phillips-curve and policy-rule analysis). HP and Christiano–Fitzgerald trends of the unemployment rate are still computed and included in the downloads.

The Hamilton filter is not offered as a trend here: its fitted value is a forecast made two years earlier, so it repeats past shocks eight quarters later. Hamilton cycles are shown on the Real Business Cycles page, together with the HP and Christiano–Fitzgerald cycles.

Missing data and estimates

Occasionally a source does not publish a value for a period — for example, the October 2025 household survey was not collected because of the federal government shutdown. The economy did not stop in that month, and gaps break time-series and econometric analysis, so such values are estimated here and flagged rather than left blank.

Method. An isolated gap of up to three consecutive periods is filled by linear interpolation between the published values on either side; for series measured in levels the interpolation is in logs, so growth is constant across the gap. Longer gaps are not filled automatically. Estimates in an input also flag the measures built from it (for example, the GDP deflator enters inflation, the real interest rate and real debt).

In the downloads. The raw CSV keeps exactly what the source published, including the gap. The clean CSV and MATLAB files contain the estimate, with an estimated column (1 = estimated) so that it can be excluded or treated differently in your own analysis.
