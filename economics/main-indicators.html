Definitions and methods

Series and frequencies

Each chart shows the full history published by the original agency, at its own frequency: national accounts, hours, productivity and compensation are quarterly; the unemployment rate, population and interest rates are monthly. Real series are in chained 2017 dollars or are indexes (2017 = 100), as published.

Chained-dollar components, such as durable and nondurable consumption, are not additive: they do not sum exactly to total real consumption.

Hours, productivity and real hourly compensation cover the nonfarm business sector, as published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Real hourly compensation is deflated by consumer prices. Population is the civilian noninstitutional population aged 16 and over, not the total population.

Inflation and real interest rates

Inflation is the annualised quarter-on-quarter change in the GDP price deflator, 400 × [ln(Pt) − ln(Pt−1)], in percent.

The real rate chart can be shown at an annual rate (default) or per quarter. The quarterly rate is the annual rate divided by four: the nominal rate earned over one quarter minus the quarterly log change in the GDP deflator, 100 × [ln(Pt+1) − ln(Pt)]. It is the unit used when comparing data with quarterly models.

Ex-post real interest rates are the quarterly average of the monthly nominal rate (quarters with all three months only) minus inflation realised over the following quarter, both at annual rates and in percentage points. They measure the real return actually earned, not the rate expected when the loan or bill was taken out. The Treasury bill rate is close to a risk-free rate; the prime rate is a bank lending rate set administratively.

Corporate debt

Debt securities and loans of nonfinancial corporate businesses (Federal Reserve Financial Accounts), divided by the GDP price deflator to express it in 2017 prices. This is the author’s construction, not an official real series.

Trends

Trends are estimated on 100 × the natural logarithm of each level series and converted back to the series’ own units. They are statistical estimates, not observed data, and are only shown for series measured in levels.

  • HP: Hodrick–Prescott filter, λ = 1600. Smooth and two-sided: its latest values are revised as new data arrive.
  • Hamilton: the value predicted from the four quarters known two years earlier (h = 8, p = 4). It uses only past data, is not smooth, and starts 11 quarters into the sample.
  • Christiano–Fitzgerald: the series minus its 6–32 quarter business-cycle component. It still contains fluctuations shorter than six quarters, so it is less smooth than the HP trend; its latest values are also revised.

The matching cyclical components are shown on the Real Business Cycles page.

Recession shading and data revisions

Shaded bands show US recessions from the NBER-based quarterly recession indicator published on FRED (USRECQ).

Official statistics are revised after their first release. Each update of this page uses the latest available history, so earlier values can change.

Missing data and estimates

Occasionally a source does not publish a value for a period — for example, the October 2025 unemployment rate was not collected because of the federal government shutdown. The economy did not stop in that month, and gaps break time-series and econometric analysis, so such values are estimated here and flagged rather than left blank.

Method. An isolated gap of up to three consecutive periods is filled by linear interpolation between the published values on either side. For series measured in levels the interpolation is in logs, so growth is constant across the gap. Longer gaps are not filled automatically. Estimated values are marked with a hollow circle on the charts. Estimates in an input, such as the GDP deflator, also flag the periods of the measures built from it: inflation, real interest rates and real debt.

In the downloads. The raw CSV keeps exactly what the source published, including the gap. The clean CSV and MATLAB files contain the estimate, with an estimated column (1 = estimated) so that it can be excluded or treated differently in your own analysis.
