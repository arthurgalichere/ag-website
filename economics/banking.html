Banks and Credit Supply

The lending of US commercial banks since 1950: how much credit they extend and to whom, how they fund it, how leveraged they are, the terms on which they lend and the losses they take, and how all of these move over the business cycle.

Bank credit

Assets of all commercial banks in the United States (Federal Reserve H.8), seasonally adjusted, deflated by the consumer price index (2017 prices) and averaged over each quarter. Bank credit is loans and leases plus securities.

Bank leverage

Bank capital limits lending: when losses deplete it, banks cut credit, which amplifies downturns (Bernanke and Gertler, 1995; Gertler and Kiyotaki, 2010). Adrian and Shin (2010) find that the leverage of security broker-dealers rises in booms; the book leverage of commercial banks, shown here, moves much less.

Lending rates and standards

The prime rate is the rate that banks post as the base for many business and consumer loans. The Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey asks banks each quarter whether they have tightened or eased their lending standards and how demand has changed: the net percentage is the share tightening (or seeing stronger demand) minus the share easing (or seeing weaker demand).

Credit quality

Delinquent loans are 30 days or more past due or no longer accruing interest; charge-offs are loans written off as losses, net of recoveries, at an annual rate. Both are percentages of loans, from banks’ Call Reports.

Cyclical components

Bank lending moves with output but adjusts with a lag, while lending standards and loan losses are countercyclical. Each quarterly series is filtered on its full history; each chart compares its cyclical component with that of output (real GDP), drawn in red. The statistics use the quarters each variable shares with output, from 1950 Q1 or its first quarter.

Leads and lags with output

Correlation of each variable’s cyclical component in quarter t + k with that of output in quarter t. The peak is the k with the largest correlation in absolute value: a negative k means the variable leads output, a positive k that it lags (Kydland and Prescott, 1990).

Definitions, sources and methods

Data
  • Bank credit and loans (Federal Reserve H.8, all commercial banks, seasonally adjusted, monthly): bank credit (LOANINV), loans and leases (LOANS), securities (INVEST), commercial and industrial loans (BUSLOANS), real-estate loans (REALLN), consumer loans (CONSUMER), deposits (DPSACBM027SBOG), total assets (TLAACBM027SBOG) and the residual of assets less liabilities (RALACBM027SBOG). The H.8 series are based on weekly reports and benchmarked to the quarterly Call Reports.
  • M2 (Federal Reserve H.6, M2SL), seasonally adjusted.
  • Rates: the bank prime loan rate (MPRIME) and the effective federal funds rate (FEDFUNDS), monthly averages (Federal Reserve H.15).
  • Lending standards and demand (Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey on Bank Lending Practices, quarterly): standards for commercial and industrial loans to large and middle-market firms (DRTSCILM) and to small firms (DRTSCIS), for credit card loans (DRTSCLCC), and demand for C&I loans from large and middle-market firms (DRSDCILM). The survey is taken in the first month of each quarter.
  • Delinquency and charge-off rates (Federal Reserve, from the FFIEC Call Reports, all commercial banks, seasonally adjusted, quarterly): all loans (DRALACBS, CORALACBS), business loans (DRBLACBS), single-family residential mortgages (DRSFRMACBS) and credit card loans (DRCCLACBS).

Real series divide each nominal value by the consumer price index for all urban consumers (CPIAUCSL) and multiply by its 2017 average, so they are in 2017 prices; this is the author’s construction. Bank leverage and the prime-rate spread are also the author’s calculations.

Cyclical components and statistics

Monthly series are averaged over each complete quarter. Real credit, loans, deposits and M2 are filtered as \(100 \times \ln x_t\), so their cycles are percent deviations from trend; leverage, rates, survey balances and loss rates are filtered in levels. The filters are those of the Real Business Cycles page: Hodrick–Prescott (λ = 1600), Hamilton (2018; h = 8, p = 4) and Christiano–Fitzgerald (2003; 6–32 quarters), each applied to the full history and shown from 1950 or the series’ first quarter.

Missing data and estimates

Where a source did not publish a value for a period, the value is estimated and flagged rather than left blank, because gaps break time-series and econometric analysis. An isolated gap of up to three consecutive periods is filled by linear interpolation between the published values on either side, in logs for series measured in levels (constant growth across the gap). Estimates in an input also flag the measures built from it. The downloads mark them in an _estimated column (1 = estimated).
