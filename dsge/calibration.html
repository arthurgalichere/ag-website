Calibration

Calibrating a model means choosing its parameters so that it reproduces chosen features of the data, usually long-run averages such as the investment share of output or the average interest rate, and then asking how well it accounts for everything else, such as business-cycle fluctuations (Kydland and Prescott, 1982; Cooley and Prescott, 1995). This page collects, for the models of the site, every parameter with its meaning, value and source, and the US data targets they are calibrated to, computed from the data of the site and updated with it.

How the parameters relate to the data

From targets to parameters: the steady state of the RBC model

On the balanced growth path of the canonical RBC model (King and Rebelo, 1999), output, consumption, investment and capital per person grow at the rate \(g_X\) of labour-augmenting technology, and hours per person are constant. Per-capita output growth in the data therefore measures \(g_X\); the model pages instead estimate \(g_X\) from the trend of TFP, \(g_X = g_{\mathrm{TFP}}/(1-\alpha)\), about 1.6 percent a year, against 2.0 percent for output per person since 1950; the switch above the table of implied parameters compares the two. The steady state of the detrended economy gives four relations between parameters and targets, each read in one direction to calibrate:

\[ \frac{1+g_X}{\beta} = 1 + r , \qquad \frac{I}{K} = g_X + \delta , \qquad r + \delta = \alpha\,\frac{Y}{K} , \qquad \theta\,\frac{C}{1-h} = (1-\alpha)\,\frac{Y}{h} . \]
  • Discount factor: the Euler equation with logarithmic utility, \(\beta = (1+g_X)/(1+r)\), with \(r\) the quarterly real return on capital.
  • Depreciation rate: the law of motion of capital, \(\delta = (I/Y)/(K/Y) - g_X\), with quarterly \(I/Y\) and \(K/Y\).
  • Capital’s share: the marginal product of capital equals its user cost, \(\alpha = (r + \delta)\,K/Y\). With competitive markets, \(\alpha\) is also capital’s share of income, its usual direct measure.
  • Weight of leisure: labour supply, \(\theta = (1-\alpha)(Y/C)(1-h)/h\), sets hours \(h\) at the target share of time.

The targets depend on what the data are taken to measure. The model has no government and no trade, so its investment share corresponds to \(I/(C+I)\) in the data rather than \(I/Y\); its capital stock may be all private fixed assets or only nonresidential ones, which changes \(K/Y\), and with it \(\delta\) and \(\alpha\) (Cooley and Prescott, 1995).

The discount factor and the risk-free rate

In the RBC model there is one return: capital, a bond and a share in the firm all pay \(r\). The data have several. King and Rebelo (1999) target a real return on capital of about 6.5 percent a year, close to the average real return on stocks; with per-capita growth, it implies a discount factor near their 0.984. The average real return on Treasury bills, about 1 percent a year, implies a discount factor above one: with growth, households would have to value the future more than the present to accept such a low return. This is the risk-free rate puzzle (Weil, 1989), the counterpart of the equity premium puzzle (Mehra and Prescott, 1985): models without risk premia cannot match both returns, and must choose which one \(r\) stands for. The RBC models of this site use the stock return: in them, a share is a claim on the capital stock, so the return on shares is the return on capital. Their discount factor is therefore not set by hand but computed at each update from this target and each model’s own Euler equation (with log utility, \(\beta = (1+g_X)/(1+r)\), at the trend growth of the default technology series); the weight of leisure is then set so that hours take 20 percent of available time. A model with a different Euler equation, for example with an exit rate of firms, implies a different \(\beta\) for the same target.

Stock prices and dividends in the RBC model

On The RBC Model with a Stock Market, the firm owns the capital stock and pays out what it does not invest: the stock price is the value of next period’s capital, \(p_t = K_{t+1}\), and dividends are \(d_t = \alpha Y_t - I_t\). In the steady state the price–dividend ratio follows from the great ratios, \(P/D = (K/Y)\,/\,(\alpha - I/Y)\) with quarterly \(K/Y\) (divided by four for annual dividends), and dividends are a share \(\alpha - I/Y\) of output. Comparing these with the data targets, the price–dividend ratio of the S&P 500 and the dividends of all US corporations relative to GDP, is a test of the model: no parameter is set to match them.

The New Keynesian model

The structural parameters of The Canonical New Keynesian Model (page coming soon) are those of Arthur Galichère’s notes. The slope of the Phillips curve depends on price stickiness: with Calvo pricing, \(\kappa = (1-\xi)(1-\xi\beta)/\xi\), and a price lasts \(1/(1-\xi)\) quarters on average, about ten months for \(\xi = 0.7\), in line with the frequency of price changes in US consumer prices (Nakamura and Steinsson, 2008); with Rotemberg pricing, \(\kappa = (\varepsilon-1)/\omega\). The model has zero inflation in the steady state, so average inflation and the average federal funds rate are removed from the data it is compared with. The persistences and sizes of its four shocks are estimated on Extracting Shocks for the New Keynesian Model: the persistences are those that the shocks backed out from the data reproduce.

Stock-market moments for a model of stock-market dynamics

A model of the stock market is usually judged on a standard set of moments: the average and volatility of real stock returns and of the real risk-free rate, the equity premium, and the level, volatility and persistence of the price–dividend ratio, with the growth of dividends and consumption (Campbell and Cochrane, 1999; Bansal and Yaron, 2004). The targets marked for the forthcoming stock-market dynamics model are these moments. Stock prices move far more than dividends (Shiller, 1981), and the price–dividend ratio is very persistent, which is why high valuations predict low returns rather than high dividend growth (Cochrane, 2008). Total payouts, including share buybacks, will be added.

Firm entry and exit. In the stock-market-dynamics model, a firm survives each quarter with probability \(s\), entrants issue new shares to finance their capital, and the price of a cohort of firms relative to the cohort one quarter older is \(\kappa\), with \(s < \kappa < 1\). In the steady state, the market value of all firms is \(p^A = m\,p_0\) with \(m = \kappa(1-s)/(\kappa-s)\), entrants issue \(I_0 = (1-s)\,p_0\), so that new issues are a share \(I_0/p^A = 1 - s/\kappa\) of market value, and the household’s Euler equation gives \(\kappa = s\,(1/\beta - d/p^A)^{-1}\). With \(s\) from the Business Dynamics page, \(\kappa\) can therefore be set either from gross equity issues relative to market value or from the dividend yield: the table of implied parameters shows both. Shocks to \(\kappa\) shift the value of young firms relative to old ones; the investor sentiment index of Baker and Wurgler, built from the pricing of young and hard-to-value stocks, is their empirical counterpart, and its persistence is a benchmark for \(\rho_\kappa\) (see Investor Sentiment and Equity Issuance).

Data and samples

The targets are computed from the data of the site: great ratios and growth rates from Main Economic Indicators (Bureau of Economic Analysis, via FRED), interest rates and inflation from the same page, and stock-market moments from Main Stock Market Indicators (Robert J. Shiller’s data; corporate dividends from the national accounts). Each target is the average over all the quarters (or years) available, shown in its row. Growth rates are annualised quarterly log changes; the per-capita measure divides output, consumption and dividends by the population. Annual returns are four times the average quarterly return, and their volatility twice the standard deviation of quarterly returns.
