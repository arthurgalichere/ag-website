A Guide to Writing Your Literature Review

The literature review is one of the most important sections of your dissertation as it places your project in the literature and highlights the novelty of your research. Despite being an important element of the dissertation, many students overlook this section. This note will provide you with a simple and efficient method on how to elaborate your literature review.

Once you have a vague research topic in mind, the constitution of your literature review starts right away. In other words, you are going to gather information, and thus start your literature review, while you are narrowing down your research question.

When you have settled on a research topic, you are going to narrow your research question by examining what other researchers have done. That is to say, you are going to read papers. This is the crucial moment when you should not waste time and take some notes on the work of others. By collecting the essential information for every paper you are reading related to your research topic, you are constituting your own literature review.

What is the essential information you should collect? For every paper, you need to write down at least the author(s), the year of publication (or the year of the current version of the working paper), the research question, the answer to this research question and the methodology. This gives you a useful record of what each paper does. On its own, however, it only describes the literature: it does not yet give you a critical perspective on it.

To write a critical literature review, you need to understand how and why each paper reaches its conclusions, and how far you can trust them. When reading a paper, work through the following stages and note what you find at each one:

  1. Research question. Identify and clearly state the question the paper asks.

  2. Methodology. Explain the methodological framework: the model, the empirical strategy, or both.

  3. Contribution. Identify the paper’s contribution and what is new relative to the existing literature.

  4. Mechanisms. Trace the main mechanisms and the key factors driving the results.

  5. Assumptions. Critically assess the main assumptions: are they realistic, and how much do the results depend on them?

  6. Policy implications. Note what the results imply for policy, if anything, and how robust these implications are.

  7. Conclusions. State the paper’s conclusions and its central takeaway: the answer to the research question.

Additionally, and crucially if you are doing an empirical project, you should note what data was used and where it was collected. This will be tremendously helpful for your project since you will not waste time searching for what data you should use and where to get it. Finally, you can include a final column called comments where you write about the paper’s specific details, such as particular variables, important theoretical mechanisms, or just some observations.

Once you have read enough papers related to your research question, you should have enough information to directly write down your literature review! And if you missed an important point, you know where to look for it!

When writing your literature review, you must demonstrate how the reviewed works directly connect to your research topic. Put differently, you should present the existing literature with a clear connection to your well-defined research topic. Instead of merely listing viewpoints or findings, a critical analysis of each perspective is necessary. This involves:

  • Differentiating between existing studies and gaps that require attention. Explore the underlying reasons behind each viewpoint

  • Comparing arguments across different papers, assessing how one paper’s argument compares to another’s

  • Highlighting areas that demand further exploration and identify inconsistencies or puzzles in the current literature

  • Examining the predominant methodologies and research techniques employed in the field, briefly outlining their respective strengths and weaknesses. For theoretical papers, this means retrieving and explaining the key mechanisms that generate the study’s results

The last question is how to organise your literature review: Chronologically? Or by topics? Maybe an alternative way? There is no right answer to this question as different projects will be more adequate to one style or another. Then I will leave this answer to your own judgment.
