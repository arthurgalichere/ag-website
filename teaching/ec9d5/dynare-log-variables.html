Interpreting Dynamic Variables in Log-Linear Macroeconomic Models

On this course, every variable in a Dynare model is a logarithm and every shock is measured in percent. A number in Dynare’s output can then be a log level, a deviation in percent, or, for an interest rate, a change in percentage points. This page explains what each object measures, how to move between levels, logs and percentages, and how to read Dynare’s steady state and impulse responses correctly.

1. Levels and Logs

Let \(Y_t^{\text{level}}\) denote output in period \(t\), measured in its natural units. Its (natural) logarithm, written with a lower-case letter, is

Definition \[ y_t=\log\!\left(Y_t^{\text{level}}\right) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Y_t^{\text{level}}=\exp(y_t). \]

The logarithm turns a positive level into a log variable; the exponential reverses it. Throughout, \(\log\) is the natural logarithm, the same as log in Dynare and MATLAB. The same convention applies to every other variable: \(c_t\) is log consumption, \(i_t\) log investment, \(k_t\) log capital, and so on.

Worked example

If output is 100, the model variable is

\[ y_t=\log(100)\approx 4.605. \]

Conversely, if the model gives \(y_t=4.605\), output in levels is \(\exp(4.605)\approx 100\).

The model variable \(y_t\) is not output: it is the logarithm of output. A value of 4.605 means output of about 100, not 4.605 units of output.

2. The Steady State in Levels and Logs

The same distinction applies to the steady state. If \(\bar Y^{\text{level}}\) is steady-state output, its logarithm is

\[ \bar y=\log\!\left(\bar Y^{\text{level}}\right) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bar Y^{\text{level}}=\exp(\bar y). \]

If steady-state output is 100, then \(\bar Y^{\text{level}}=100\) and \(\bar y=\log(100)\approx4.605\). When a variable is written in logs in the model, its steady state is in logs too. The two representations carry the same information, but they must not be mixed.

In Dynare the steady state of y appears in two places, and both are log values:

  • inside the model block, the operator STEADY_STATE(y) stands for \(\bar y\);
  • after steady; or stoch_simul has run, the values are stored in oo_.steady_state, in the order of the variable names in M_.endo_names.

3. Three Ways to Measure a Deviation

Once the variable and its steady state are defined, we can measure how far the variable is from its steady state. There are three common measures. They answer the same question in different units, so the numbers differ. As a running example, output rises from its steady state of 100 to 102.

3.1. Deviation in levels

\[ Y_t^{\text{level}}-\bar Y^{\text{level}} \]

This is an absolute difference, in the units of the variable itself (a unit deviation). In the example, \(102-100=2\): output is 2 units above its steady state.

3.2. Log deviation

The log deviation is the difference between the log of the variable and the log of its steady state. Because the difference of two logs is the log of a ratio,

Log deviation \[ y_t-\bar y =\log\!\left(Y_t^{\text{level}}\right)-\log\!\left(\bar Y^{\text{level}}\right) =\log\!\left(\frac{Y_t^{\text{level}}}{\bar Y^{\text{level}}}\right). \]

It therefore measures the relative difference between the variable and its steady state. In the example, \(\log(102)-\log(100)=\log(1.02)\approx0.0198\). This is not 0.0198 units of output: it is a difference between two logarithms, a pure number with no units.

3.3. Percentage deviation

\[ 100\times\frac{Y_t^{\text{level}}-\bar Y^{\text{level}}}{\bar Y^{\text{level}}} \]

In the example, \(100\times(102-100)/100=2\%\): output is 2% above its steady state. The percentage deviation can be computed from the log deviation alone. Taking the exponential of the log deviation gives the ratio \(Y_t^{\text{level}}/\bar Y^{\text{level}}=\exp(y_t-\bar y)\), so

Exact percentage deviation \[ 100\times\frac{Y_t^{\text{level}}-\bar Y^{\text{level}}}{\bar Y^{\text{level}}} =100\times\left[\exp(y_t-\bar y)-1\right]. \]

3.4. The three measures side by side

4. Log Deviations and Percentages

For small deviations, the log deviation is approximately the percentage deviation written as a decimal. The reason is the first-order approximation \(\exp(x)\approx1+x\) for small \(x\). Applied to the exact formula of section 3.3,

\[ 100\times\left[\exp(y_t-\bar y)-1\right]\;\approx\;100\times(y_t-\bar y). \]
Rule of thumb \[ \text{Percentage deviation}\;\approx\;100\times\text{log deviation}. \]

This is why a log deviation of 0.01 is read as “about 1% above the steady state”. It is an approximation, and its accuracy depends on the size of the deviation:

For business-cycle movements of a few per cent, the approximation is very close (the highlighted row is the running example). For large movements it is not: \(100x\) understates rises and overstates falls, and the error grows with the size of the deviation. A log deviation of 0.20 is a rise of about 22%, not 20%.

Why models use logs

Log deviations are symmetric and add up over time. If output rises from 100 to 102 and then falls back to 100, the two log changes are \(+0.0198\) and \(-0.0198\), which sum to zero. In percentages the same path is \(+2\%\) and then \(-1.96\%\). Logs also turn products into sums, so a Cobb–Douglas production function becomes linear in logs (section 6).

5. Reading Dynare’s Output

5.1. Shocks in percent, deviations in percent

On this course, shock standard deviations are set in percent: stderr 0.89 means a 0.89% shock, not 0.0089. With a first-order approximation (stoch_simul(order = 1)), the solution is linear in the shocks, so scaling every shock by 100 scales every deviation from the steady state by 100. Dynare’s deviations of a log variable are then 100 times log deviations, that is, percentage deviations in the sense of section 4.

Reading rule (shocks in percent, order = 1) \[ \text{Dynare's deviation of } y \;=\;100\,(y_t-\bar y) \;=\;100\times\log\!\left(\frac{Y_t^{\text{level}}}{\bar Y^{\text{level}}}\right) \;\approx\;\text{percentage deviation of output}. \]

The response of y to the shock eps_A is stored in oo_.irfs.y_eps_A, as a deviation from the steady state. A value of 0.5 means output is about 0.5% above its steady state: no multiplication by 100 is needed. The same holds for simulated series minus their steady state, and for the moments Dynare reports.

Only at order = 1. The rescaling works because the first-order solution is linear. At order = 2 or higher, the size of the shocks changes the solution itself (through risk terms such as the variance), so shocks must then be in log units.
In other papers and code. Many models set shocks in log units instead (stderr 0.0089). Their impulse responses are then log deviations (0.005 for a 0.5% deviation), and must be multiplied by 100 before they are read as percentages. At order = 1 the two set-ups give the same economics; only the units differ.

5.2. Interest rates and inflation

All endogenous variables are in logs, including gross rates: \(r_t=\log(1+R_t)\) for a net rate \(R_t\). Because \(\log(1+R_t)-\log(1+\bar R)\approx R_t-\bar R\), the deviation of a log gross rate is the change in the net rate itself. With shocks in percent it is read in percentage points per period, never as a percentage change of the rate.

Worked example

In a quarterly model, the impulse response of the log gross interest rate is 0.25 on impact. The interest rate is 0.25 percentage points higher per quarter, or about \(4\times0.25=1\) percentage point higher at an annual rate. It is not 0.25% higher than its steady-state value.

6. Writing the Model in Dynare

Every endogenous variable is declared in logs, and every equation is written in levels using exp(). The equations then read like the model in the notes, and Dynare’s first-order approximation is automatically in log deviations. Exogenous processes are log AR(1) processes, written directly in logs.

Capital follows Dynare’s end-of-period convention: k is \(\log K_{t+1}\), chosen in period \(t\), so production uses k(-1). With these conventions, each expression measures:

Dynare expressions and what they measure, with variables in logs and shocks in percent
Expression What it measures
yLog output, \(y_t\)
exp(y)Output in levels, in the model equations
STEADY_STATE(y)Steady-state log output, \(\bar y\)
exp(STEADY_STATE(y))Steady-state output in levels, \(\bar Y^{\text{level}}\)
y - STEADY_STATE(y)Deviation from the steady state: in percent at order = 1 with shocks in percent
y - y(-1)Growth of output from \(t-1\) to \(t\), in percent
Do not add 100* in front of y - STEADY_STATE(y): with shocks in percent, the deviation is already in percent, and the factor would put it in hundredths of a percent.

6.1. Exact numbers after solving

Dynare’s percent deviations are 100 times log deviations, so the rule of thumb of section 4 applies. For large responses, or to report results in levels, convert them after the model has been solved. Do not write these conversions inside the model block: at order = 1, Dynare linearises every equation, so an “exact” formula there gives the same numbers as the approximate one.

For business-cycle-sized responses the difference is negligible (see the table in section 4). It matters for large shocks, and whenever you report results in levels.

7. Rules to Remember

Whenever a model variable is the logarithm of a level, \(y_t=\log(Y_t^{\text{level}})\), distinguish the model variable from the economic quantity it represents, and know the units of the shocks.

  • y is log output; exp(y) is output in levels. Write the equations in levels with exp().
  • STEADY_STATE(y) and the value in oo_.steady_state are steady-state log output; their exponential is steady-state output in levels.
  • Shock standard deviations are in percent (stderr 0.89 for a 0.89% shock). At order = 1, every deviation, impulse response and moment is then in percent.
  • The deviation of a log gross rate is a change in the rate in percentage points per period; multiply by 4 to annualise a quarterly rate.
  • The percent rescaling is valid only at order = 1; at higher orders, shocks must be in log units.
  • Compute exact transformations after solving the model, not inside the model block.
