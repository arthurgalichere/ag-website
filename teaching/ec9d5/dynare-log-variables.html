Interpreting Dynamic Variables in Log-Linear Macroeconomic Models

Many Dynare models write their variables in logarithms. A number such as 0.02 in the output can then mean a log deviation, roughly a 2% deviation, or, for an interest rate, 2 percentage points. This page explains what each object measures, how to move between levels, logs and percentages, and how to read Dynare’s steady state and impulse responses correctly.

1. Levels and Logs

Let \(Y_t^{\text{level}}\) denote output in period \(t\), measured in its natural units. Its (natural) logarithm, written with a lower-case letter, is

Definition \[ y_t=\log\!\left(Y_t^{\text{level}}\right) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Y_t^{\text{level}}=\exp(y_t). \]

The logarithm turns a positive level into a log variable; the exponential reverses it. Throughout, \(\log\) is the natural logarithm, the same as log in Dynare and MATLAB. The same convention applies to every other variable: \(c_t\) is log consumption, \(i_t\) log investment, \(k_t\) log capital, and so on.

Worked example

If output is 100, the model variable is

\[ y_t=\log(100)\approx 4.605. \]

Conversely, if the model gives \(y_t=4.605\), output in levels is \(\exp(4.605)\approx 100\).

The model variable \(y_t\) is not output: it is the logarithm of output. A value of 4.605 means output of about 100, not 4.605 units of output.

2. The Steady State in Levels and Logs

The same distinction applies to the steady state. If \(\bar Y^{\text{level}}\) is steady-state output, its logarithm is

\[ \bar y=\log\!\left(\bar Y^{\text{level}}\right) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bar Y^{\text{level}}=\exp(\bar y). \]

If steady-state output is 100, then \(\bar Y^{\text{level}}=100\) and \(\bar y=\log(100)\approx4.605\). When a variable is written in logs in the model, its steady state is in logs too. The two representations carry the same information, but they must not be mixed.

In Dynare the steady state of y appears in two places, and both are log values:

  • inside the model block, the operator STEADY_STATE(y) stands for \(\bar y\);
  • after steady; or stoch_simul has run, the values are stored in oo_.steady_state, in the order of the variable names in M_.endo_names.

3. Three Ways to Measure a Deviation

Once the variable and its steady state are defined, we can measure how far the variable is from its steady state. There are three common measures. They answer the same question in different units, so the numbers differ. As a running example, output rises from its steady state of 100 to 102.

3.1. Deviation in levels

\[ Y_t^{\text{level}}-\bar Y^{\text{level}} \]

This is an absolute difference, in the units of the variable itself (a unit deviation). In the example, \(102-100=2\): output is 2 units above its steady state.

3.2. Log deviation

The log deviation is the difference between the log of the variable and the log of its steady state. Because the difference of two logs is the log of a ratio,

Log deviation \[ y_t-\bar y =\log\!\left(Y_t^{\text{level}}\right)-\log\!\left(\bar Y^{\text{level}}\right) =\log\!\left(\frac{Y_t^{\text{level}}}{\bar Y^{\text{level}}}\right). \]

It therefore measures the relative difference between the variable and its steady state. In the example, \(\log(102)-\log(100)=\log(1.02)\approx0.0198\). This is not 0.0198 units of output: it is a difference between two logarithms, a pure number with no units.

3.3. Percentage deviation

\[ 100\times\frac{Y_t^{\text{level}}-\bar Y^{\text{level}}}{\bar Y^{\text{level}}} \]

In the example, \(100\times(102-100)/100=2\%\): output is 2% above its steady state. The percentage deviation can be computed from the log deviation alone. Taking the exponential of the log deviation gives the ratio \(Y_t^{\text{level}}/\bar Y^{\text{level}}=\exp(y_t-\bar y)\), so

Exact percentage deviation \[ 100\times\frac{Y_t^{\text{level}}-\bar Y^{\text{level}}}{\bar Y^{\text{level}}} =100\times\left[\exp(y_t-\bar y)-1\right]. \]

3.4. The three measures side by side

Deviation in levels \(Y_t^{\text{level}}-\bar Y^{\text{level}}\) Units of the variable 2 units

4. Log Deviations and Percentages

For small deviations, the log deviation is approximately the percentage deviation written as a decimal. The reason is the first-order approximation \(\exp(x)\approx1+x\) for small \(x\). Applied to the exact formula of section 3.3,

\[ 100\times\left[\exp(y_t-\bar y)-1\right]\;\approx\;100\times(y_t-\bar y). \]
Rule of thumb \[ \text{Percentage deviation}\;\approx\;100\times\text{log deviation}. \]

This is why a log deviation of 0.01 is read as “about 1% above the steady state”. It is an approximation, and its accuracy depends on the size of the deviation:

For business-cycle movements of a few per cent, the approximation is very close (the highlighted row is the running example). For large movements it is not: \(100x\) understates rises and overstates falls, and the error grows with the size of the deviation. A log deviation of 0.20 is a rise of about 22%, not 20%.

Why models use logs

Log deviations are symmetric and add up over time. If output rises from 100 to 102 and then falls back to 100, the two log changes are \(+0.0198\) and \(-0.0198\), which sum to zero. In percentages the same path is \(+2\%\) and then \(-1.96\%\). Logs also turn products into sums, so a Cobb–Douglas production function becomes linear in logs (section 6).

5. Reading Dynare’s Output

5.1. Impulse responses and simulations

With a first-order approximation (stoch_simul(order=1)), Dynare reports impulse responses as deviations from the steady state, in the units in which the variable is declared. The response of y to the shock eps_a is stored in oo_.irfs.y_eps_a. Simulated series are reported in levels of the model variable, so subtracting the steady state gives the same kind of deviation.

If y is log output, its impulse response is a log deviation: a value of 0.004 means output is about 0.4% above its steady state. Dynare’s IRF plots show these raw numbers; multiply them by 100 before labelling an axis “% deviation from steady state”.

5.2. Rates are not logged

Interest rates, inflation and the unemployment rate are usually written in levels, as decimals. In a quarterly model, \(r_t=0.01\) is a 1% quarterly rate. Their deviation \(r_t-\bar r\) is then a change in the rate itself, read in percentage points, never as a percentage change of the rate.

Worked example

The impulse response of a quarterly interest rate is 0.0025 on impact. The rate is \(100\times0.0025=0.25\) percentage points higher per quarter, or about \(400\times0.0025=1\) percentage point higher at an annual rate. It is not 0.25% higher than its steady-state value.

5.3. The loglinear option

An alternative to writing the model in logs is to write it in levels and run stoch_simul(order=1, loglinear). Dynare then approximates the model in the logs of all variables, so every reported response is a log deviation and can be read with the rule of thumb of section 4. All steady-state values must then be strictly positive.

Watch the rates. The loglinear option logs every variable, including interest rates and inflation. Their responses then become percentage changes of the rate (for example 25% of a 1% rate), not changes in percentage points. Many researchers therefore write the model in logs by hand and keep rates in levels.

y - STEADY_STATE(y) does not give the deviation of output in levels. It gives the log deviation.

6.1. Exact formulas and first-order solutions

Suppose you add a variable to the model block to report the exact percentage deviation, such as y_pct = 100*(exp(y - STEADY_STATE(y)) - 1);. With order=1, Dynare linearises every equation around the steady state, including this one. Its first-order approximation is \(100(y_t-\bar y)\), so y_pct gives exactly the same numbers as the approximate formula. Likewise, exp(y) - exp(STEADY_STATE(y)) becomes \(\exp(\bar y)\,(y_t-\bar y)\).

7. Rules to Remember

Whenever a model variable is the logarithm of a level, \(y_t=\log(Y_t^{\text{level}})\), distinguish the model variable from the economic quantity it represents.

  • y is log output; exp(y) is output in levels.
  • STEADY_STATE(y) and the value in oo_.steady_state are steady-state log output; their exponential is steady-state output in levels.
  • y - STEADY_STATE(y) is the log deviation; 100*(y - STEADY_STATE(y)) is approximately the percentage deviation; 100*(exp(y - STEADY_STATE(y)) - 1) is the exact percentage deviation.
  • Dynare’s impulse responses are deviations from the steady state in the units of the variable: log deviations for logged variables, changes in the rate for rates.
  • At order=1, transformations written inside the model block are linearised. Compute exact transformations after solving the model.
  • Keep the variable, its steady state and its deviation on the same scale: all in logs, or all in levels.
