Uncertainty, the Euler Equation, and Precautionary Saving

This page introduces the isoelastic utility function, solves the two-period consumption–saving problem under certainty, then adds a mean-preserving income risk. The interactive graphs separate the immediate effect of the risk on expected marginal utility from the household’s equilibrium saving response.

1. The Isoelastic Utility Function

Preferences

Isoelastic utility

\[ u(c)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{c^{1-\gamma}-1}{1-\gamma}, & \gamma\neq1,\\[1ex] \ln(c), & \gamma=1, \end{cases} \qquad \gamma>0, \] \[ u'(c)=c^{-\gamma}>0, \qquad u''(c)=-\gamma c^{-\gamma-1}<0, \] \[ u'''(c)=\gamma(\gamma+1)c^{-\gamma-2}>0, \qquad -\frac{c\,u''(c)}{u'(c)}=\gamma . \]

Utility is increasing and concave. The parameter \(\gamma\) determines its curvature, and that curvature, \(-c\,u''(c)/u'(c)\), is the same at every level of consumption, hence “isoelastic”. Every curve passes through \(u(1)=0\) with slope \(u'(1)=1\), so the graph isolates the effect of \(\gamma\).

Interpretation

What \(\gamma\) measures

Over time: a dynamic problem under certainty
\(\gamma\) is the inverse of the elasticity of intertemporal substitution, \(\text{EIS}=1/\gamma\). It sets the curvature of the instantaneous utility function, and therefore of the value function, and measures how strongly the household wants to smooth consumption across periods.
Across states: uncertainty
\(\gamma\) is the coefficient of relative risk aversion, \(-c\,u''(c)/u'(c)\). The same curvature now measures how much the household dislikes consumption that varies across states of the world, hence the name constant relative risk aversion (CRRA).
Both at once: a dynamic problem with uncertainty
One parameter does both jobs. Choosing a degree of risk aversion fixes the EIS at its inverse, and vice versa, although the data may call for quite different values of the two. Recursive (Epstein–Zin) preferences relax this restriction by separating them.

The third derivative matters too: prudence, \(-c\,u'''(c)/u''(c)=\gamma+1\), drives the precautionary saving studied in sections 3 to 5.

2. Model Overview under Certainty

Household problem

Utility maximisation

\[ \max_{A}\; u(c_1)+\beta\,u(c_2) \]

The household chooses saving \(A\) in period 1. Income \(W_1\) and \(W_2\) is known with certainty, and \(u\) is any increasing, concave utility function.

Constraints

Budget constraints

\[ c_1=W_1-A, \qquad c_2=(1+r)A+W_2. \]

Saving earns the gross return \(1+r\) and is consumed, together with period-2 income, in period 2.

Constraints

Intertemporal budget constraint

\[ c_1+\frac{c_2}{1+r}=W_1+\frac{W_2}{1+r}. \]

Eliminating \(A\): the present value of consumption equals the present value of income.

Optimality condition

Euler equation

\[ -u'(c_1)+\beta(1+r)\,u'(c_2)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u'(c_1)=\beta(1+r)\,u'(c_2). \]

The marginal utility cost of saving one more unit today equals its discounted marginal utility gain tomorrow.

Optimality condition

Euler equation with isoelastic utility

\[ c_1^{-\gamma}=\beta(1+r)\,c_2^{-\gamma} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{c_2}{c_1}=\left[\beta(1+r)\right]^{1/\gamma}, \] \[ \ln\frac{c_2}{c_1}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\ln\left[\beta(1+r)\right]. \]

With no uncertainty, \(\gamma\) plays only its intertemporal role: consumption growth responds to \(\beta(1+r)\) with elasticity \(\text{EIS}=1/\gamma\). If \(\beta(1+r)=1\), consumption is perfectly smoothed; above 1 it rises over time, below 1 it falls.

The Euler equation on the marginal-utility curve

Choose \(\gamma\) and \(\beta(1+r)\). Income is \(W_1=W_2=10\) and \(r=0\), so moving \(\beta(1+r)\) changes the discount factor \(\beta\) while the budget constraint stays \(c_1+c_2=20\). Only the product \(\beta(1+r)\) enters the Euler equation.

3. Model Overview under Uncertainty

Household problem

Expected-utility maximisation

\[ \max_{A}\; u(c_1)+\beta\,\mathbb{E}\left[u(c_2^s)\right] \]

The household chooses risk-free saving \(A\) in period 1, before period-2 income \(W_2^s\) is realised in state \(s\in\{L,H\}\).

Constraints

Budget constraints in each state

\[ c_1=W_1-A, \qquad c_2^L=(1+r)A+W_2^L, \qquad c_2^H=(1+r)A+W_2^H. \]

Saving is chosen once, so the same \(A\) carries over into both future states.

Constraints

Intertemporal budget constraint

\[ c_1+\frac{c_2^s}{1+r}=W_1+\frac{W_2^s}{1+r}, \qquad s\in\{L,H\}. \]

The present value of consumption equals the present value of income in each possible state.

Income risk

Mean-preserving income risk

\[ W_2^s= \begin{cases} W-\varepsilon & \text{with probability }1/2 \quad (s=L),\\ W+\varepsilon & \text{with probability }1/2 \quad (s=H), \end{cases} \qquad \mathbb{E}[W_2^s]=W, \] \[ \mathbb{E}[c_2^s]=(1+r)A+W, \qquad c_2^L=\mathbb{E}[c_2^s]-\varepsilon, \qquad c_2^H=\mathbb{E}[c_2^s]+\varepsilon. \]

Increasing \(\varepsilon\) spreads future income and consumption without changing their expected value, as long as saving is unchanged.

Optimality condition

Euler equation under uncertainty

\[ -u'(c_1)+\beta(1+r)\,\mathbb{E}\left[u'(c_2^s)\right]=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u'(c_1)=\beta(1+r)\,\mathbb{E}\left[u'(c_2^s)\right]. \]

Marginal utility today now equals discounted expected marginal utility tomorrow.

With isoelastic utility, \(\gamma\) is now also the coefficient of relative risk aversion. For the interactive exercise below, \(\beta(1+r)=1\), so the relevant Euler equation depends only on whether \(\varepsilon\) is zero or positive.

Certainty case Active because ε = 0
\[ c_1^{-\gamma}=\mathbb{E}[c_2^s]^{-\gamma} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_1=\mathbb{E}[c_2^s]. \]
Uncertainty case Inactive because ε = 0
\[ c_1^{-\gamma} =\tfrac12\left(\mathbb{E}[c_2^s]-\varepsilon\right)^{-\gamma} +\tfrac12\left(\mathbb{E}[c_2^s]+\varepsilon\right)^{-\gamma}. \]
Jensen’s inequality: when \(\varepsilon>0\), convex marginal utility implies \[ \mathbb{E}\left[u'(c_2^s)\right]>u'\!\left(\mathbb{E}[c_2^s]\right). \] Expected marginal utility rises, so the household reduces \(c_1\) and increases saving until the uncertain Euler equation holds again. This is precautionary saving. It requires \(u'''>0\) (prudence), which isoelastic utility always satisfies.

Saving stays at its certainty level. The low and high states spread around unchanged expected consumption, and convex marginal utility pushes \(\mathbb{E}[u'(c_2^s)]\) above \(u'(\mathbb{E}[c_2^s])\). The Euler equation would then require a lower \(c_1\).

The household lowers \(c_1\) and raises \(A\). Both \(c_2^L\) and \(c_2^H\) shift to the right until current marginal utility equals expected future marginal utility.
