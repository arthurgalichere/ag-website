Linking Data to a Macroeconomic Model for Bayesian Estimation in Dynare

Bayesian estimation compares what the model predicts with what the data show. Measurement equations make that comparison possible: they turn each model variable into exactly the object stored in your data file, in the same units. This page shows how to write them for cyclical components, growth rates and interest rates, and how to check them before estimating. It builds on Part 1, which explains log deviations.

1. What a Measurement Equation Does

The model describes theoretical quantities such as productivity, capital, consumption and output. The data contain particular statistical measures of some of them. The two are rarely the same object.

Suppose the model contains log output, \(y_t=\log(Y_t^{\text{level}})\), while the data contain the cyclical component of output, in percentage points of deviation from trend. The model variable is a log level (around 4.6 if output is 100); the data fluctuate around zero. They cannot be compared directly.

A measurement equation defines a new model variable that reproduces the observed series. In Dynare it is an ordinary endogenous variable, defined by an equation in the model block and declared as observed with varobs:

On this page \(Y_t^{\text{data}}\) denotes the observed series and y_obs its model counterpart. What the measurement equation looks like depends entirely on how the data were constructed.

2. Three Questions About Your Data

Before writing a measurement equation, establish exactly what each series in the data file represents.

  1. Which economic object? A deviation from trend (cyclical component), a growth rate, a level, or a rate such as the interest rate or inflation.
  2. Which transformation? Was the series logged before filtering or differencing? Was it detrended with the Hodrick–Prescott (HP) filter? Is the cycle a log difference or an exact percentage deviation from trend?
  3. Which units? Decimals or percentage points? For rates, quarterly or annualised?

For example, “output as a percentage deviation from its HP trend” and “output growth from one quarter to the next, in per cent” are both expressed in per cent, but they are different economic objects and need different measurement equations.

3. HP-Filtered Cycles

3.1. What the cyclical component measures

The HP filter splits a series into a trend and a cyclical component. Applied to log output,

\[ \log\!\left(Y_t^{\text{level}}\right)=\log\!\left(Y_t^{\text{trend}}\right)+y_t^{\text{cycle}}, \]

so the cyclical component is the log deviation of output from its HP trend:

HP cycle of a logged series \[ y_t^{\text{cycle}} =\log\!\left(Y_t^{\text{level}}\right)-\log\!\left(Y_t^{\text{trend}}\right) =\log\!\left(\frac{Y_t^{\text{level}}}{Y_t^{\text{trend}}}\right). \]

This is the data counterpart of the log deviation from the steady state in Part 1, with one difference. The steady state is a fixed reference point implied by the model; the HP trend is a statistical estimate that moves over time. Matching the two amounts to assuming that the stationary model describes fluctuations around the trend the filter has removed.

3.2. Data in percentage log deviations

The most common construction multiplies the log cycle by 100:

\[ Y_t^{\text{data}}=100\left[\log\!\left(Y_t^{\text{level}}\right)-\log\!\left(Y_t^{\text{trend}}\right)\right]. \]

The factor of 100 expresses the log deviation in percentage points. A cycle of 0.02 is recorded as \(Y_t^{\text{data}}=2\): output about 2% above its trend.

The model counterpart uses the log deviation from the steady state, also multiplied by 100:

  • y is log output and STEADY_STATE(y) steady-state log output;
  • their difference is the log deviation from the steady state;
  • the factor of 100 puts it in percentage points, the units of the data.
Worked example

If the model produces \(y_t-\bar y=0.02\), the measurement equation gives \(100\times0.02=2\), directly comparable with an observation of 2 in data built as above.

3.3. Data in exact percentage deviations

Some datasets instead report the exact percentage deviation from the HP trend:

\[ Y_t^{\text{data}}=100\left[\frac{Y_t^{\text{level}}}{Y_t^{\text{trend}}}-1\right] =100\left[\exp\!\left(y_t^{\text{cycle}}\right)-1\right]. \]

Its model counterpart applies the same exponential transformation to the log deviation:

Standard Bayesian estimation in Dynare uses a first-order solution and the Kalman filter. Dynare then linearises the measurement equations along with the rest of the model, and the linearised form of 100*(exp(y - STEADY_STATE(y)) - 1) is 100*(y - STEADY_STATE(y)). At first order the two equations are therefore identical. The distinction only matters in nonlinear estimation (for example a second-order solution with a particle filter). The simplest consistent choice is to build the data as 100 times the log cycle (section 3.2) and use the matching equation.

3.4. Which equation to use

Measurement equations for HP-filtered data
How the data were built Measurement equation
\(100\left[\log(Y_t)-\log(Y_t^{\text{trend}})\right]\) y_obs = 100*(y - STEADY_STATE(y));
\(100\left[Y_t/Y_t^{\text{trend}}-1\right]\) y_obs = 100*(exp(y - STEADY_STATE(y)) - 1);

The rule is to reproduce in the model the statistical definition of the observed series. Two series both described as “percentage deviations” may be built differently: check how yours was actually calculated.

4. Growth Rates

Instead of HP-filtered cycles, many estimations use growth rates, which avoid filtering the data. The measurement equation must then reproduce the growth rate and deal with its mean.

4.1. Log growth

The log growth rate of output is the change in log output between two consecutive periods:

Log growth rate \[ \Delta y_t=y_t-y_{t-1}=\log\!\left(\frac{Y_t^{\text{level}}}{Y_{t-1}^{\text{level}}}\right). \]

As with deviations, for small changes the log growth rate is approximately the percentage growth rate written as a decimal.

4.4. The mean growth rate

In a stationary (detrended) model, \(y_t-y_{t-1}\) fluctuates around zero. Observed growth does not: US real GDP, for instance, has grown by around 0.5% per quarter on average in recent decades. Fitting a zero-mean model to data with a positive mean would distort the estimates. There are two standard fixes:

  • demean the data, so that the observed growth rate also fluctuates around zero; or
  • add a trend-growth parameter to the measurement equation and estimate it with the other parameters.

HP cycles do not need this step, because the filter removes the trend and the cycle has a mean close to zero.

4.5. Summary

Measurement equations for growth rates
How the data were built Measurement equation Units
\(\log(Y_t)-\log(Y_{t-1})\) y_obs = y - y(-1); Decimal log growth
\(100\left[\log(Y_t)-\log(Y_{t-1})\right]\) y_obs = 100*(y - y(-1)); Percentage log growth
\(100\left[Y_t/Y_{t-1}-1\right]\) y_obs = 100*(exp(y - y(-1)) - 1); Exact percentage growth

Each equation assumes demeaned data; otherwise add the trend-growth parameter of section 4.4.

5. Units and Scale

Even when the model and the data measure the same economic object, their numbers may be on different scales. This is one of the most common sources of error in Bayesian estimation. A log deviation of 0.02 can be written in three ways:

One log deviation of 0.02 written in three ways
Representation Formula Value
Log deviation\(y_t-\bar y\)0.02
Percentage log deviation\(100(y_t-\bar y)\)2
Exact percentage deviation\(100\left[\exp(y_t-\bar y)-1\right]\)2.0201

All three describe the same movement, but they are not numerically equal. If the data were built as percentage log deviations, an observation of 2 means a log deviation of 0.02, and the measurement equation must contain the factor of 100. Writing y_obs = y - STEADY_STATE(y); instead would make the model-implied series 100 times smaller than the data.

A factor-of-100 mismatch is not a minor error. Shock standard deviations are estimated in the model’s units (log points, so 0.01 is a 1% shock). To match data that are 100 times larger, the estimation pushes shock volatilities up by a factor of about 100, against their priors, and every other parameter estimate is distorted.

5.1. Interest rates and inflation

Rates need the same care. Suppose a quarterly model has the net interest rate \(r_t\) in decimals (\(\bar r=0.01\) is 1% per quarter), while the data are the annualised policy rate in per cent, demeaned. Since \(400\times0.01=4\), the model counterpart multiplies by 400:

If the data are not demeaned, use r_obs = 400*r; so that the model also matches the average level of the rate. Inflation works the same way.

5.2. A practical scale check

Before estimating, solve the model at your calibrated parameters with stoch_simul, which reports the theoretical standard deviation of every variable, including y_obs. Compare it with the standard deviation of the observed series. An HP cycle of quarterly real GDP built in percentage points typically has a standard deviation between 1 and 2. If the model reports about 0.015 instead, a factor of 100 is missing. Do this check for every observed variable.

6. Putting It Together in Dynare

Suppose the model contains log output and log investment, and the data are 100 times the HP log cycles of the two series.

  • Declare the measurement variables with var, like any endogenous variable, so that Dynare computes their model-implied values.
  • Name the data columns exactly as in varobs: the file must contain columns y_obs and inv_obs, in the units of the measurement equations.
  • Avoid stochastic singularity: the number of observed variables cannot exceed the number of shocks, counting any measurement errors. With fewer shocks than observables, the likelihood cannot be computed.

If the data are exact percentage deviations, or growth rates, replace the two measurement equations with the corresponding lines of sections 3.4 and 4.5; nothing else changes.

7. Checklist Before Estimating

  1. Identify the economic object. Cyclical component, growth rate, level or rate: these are different objects.
  2. Identify the transformation. Was the series logged before the HP filter or differencing? Is the cycle a log difference or an exact percentage deviation?
  3. Check the units. Decimals or percentage points? Quarterly or annualised? Does an observation of 2 mean a 2% deviation or a log deviation of 2?
  4. Deal with the mean. Demean growth rates and rates, or add a parameter for their mean.
  5. Write the measurement equation so that the model variable represents exactly the same object as the observed series.
  6. Check the scale. Compare the standard deviations reported by stoch_simul with those of the data to catch a missing factor of 100 or 400.
  7. Check the timing. For growth rates, y - y(-1) measures growth from \(t-1\) to \(t\), as in the data.
  8. Count shocks and observables. At least as many shocks (including measurement errors) as observed variables.

8. Key Principle

Measurement equations are not arbitrary transformations: they define how the theoretical model relates to the observed data. A correct measurement equation matches three things.

  • Economic meaning: the model variable and the data measure the same object.
  • Statistical construction: the model reproduces the transformation applied to the data, such as logging, HP filtering, differencing or demeaning.
  • Units: the model-implied and actual observations are on the same numerical scale.

When all three hold, the model and the data are correctly linked for Bayesian estimation.
